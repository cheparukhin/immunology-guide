Immune Surveillance and Tumor Escape

Your immune system may already have destroyed cancers you never knew you had. The tumors that reach a doctor are the ones that survived it, and they carry the marks of that selection.

In 1998, a 62-year-old woman died of a brain hemorrhage, and her kidneys were transplanted into two strangers. Sixteen years earlier, she had had a melanoma, a cancer of the skin’s pigment cells, removed, and her doctors considered her cured.

They were wrong. Within about two years, both recipients had melanoma in and around their new kidneys and no skin tumor of their own: the cancer had arrived with the organ.⁠1

The woman who received one kidney died in 2000. For the man who received the other, doctors stopped his immunosuppressive drugs — the medicines transplant patients take so the body won’t reject a foreign organ — and gave him interferon, a cytokine (an immune signaling protein) that activates immune cells. His body began rejecting the kidney, which was then removed along with the tumor: he gave up the transplant to be rid of the cancer. Two years after his melanoma was found, he was well and showed no sign of it.⁠1

For sixteen years, living melanoma cells had persisted in the donor without forming a tumor anyone noticed; something held them in check. In bodies whose immune defenses were deliberately suppressed, they grew rapidly. (In the recipients the cells were foreign, so part of what happened there is ordinary transplant rejection; the sixteen years in the donor are what need explaining.)

The likeliest explanation is her immune system. This chapter sets out the evidence, then takes up the questions the case raises: if the immune system can hold a cancer in check for sixteen years, why does anyone get cancer, and how do tumors escape?

A patrol for rogue cells#

In the late 1950s, Macfarlane Burnet (who proposed clonal selection, Chapter 3) and others suggested that one job of the immune system is to find and destroy cells that have turned cancerous, long before a tumor can be felt. Burnet called it “immunological surveillance,” now usually shortened to immunosurveillance.⁠2

The idea faced the problem set out in Chapter 6: cancer cells are not invaders but the body’s own cells, and most peptides on their MHC class I molecules are self peptides to which T cells were made tolerant in the thymus (Chapter 5). It was not obvious that the immune system would notice them.

Early experiments suggested it did not. In 1974, mice born without a thymus, and so thought to lack T cells, developed no more chemically induced tumors than normal mice, and for a quarter of a century most researchers treated immunosurveillance as a dead idea.⁠2 The mice, it later turned out, were not free of T cells.

The idea was revived around 2000. The decisive experiment, published in 2001 by Robert Schreiber’s team, used mice lacking a RAG gene, which encodes one of the pair of enzymes that cut DNA to assemble receptor genes from segments (V(D)J recombination, Chapter 3); without it, a mouse makes no T cells or B cells. Other mice could not respond to interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), the cytokine that T cells and natural killer (NK) cells secrete when they recognize a target.⁠3

The researchers injected a carcinogen, a cancer-causing chemical, under the skin. After 160 days, 58% of the RAG-deficient mice (30 of 52) had tumors, compared with 19% of normal mice (11 of 57); mice unresponsive to IFN-γ fared about as badly. As they aged, mice lacking these lymphocytes also developed more tumors without any carcinogen, especially if they could not respond to IFN-γ either.⁠3

In mice, at least, the immune system acts as a tumor suppressor that works from outside the cell, a layer of defense beyond the cell’s own growth controls and DNA repair (Chapter 6).

Go deeper Why the idea was abandoned, and revived

The physician-writer Lewis Thomas had suggested in 1959 that cellular immunity might have evolved partly to remove cancer cells, and the idea became known as the Burnet–Thomas hypothesis.⁠2

The 1974 experiment was misleading for three reasons. Its “nude” mice lack a thymus, but they are not free of T cells: a few still develop elsewhere. They also have plenty of NK cells, which can kill tumor cells without prior exposure to them. And, as later analyses pointed out, the mouse strain carried a highly active version of the enzyme that converts the carcinogen methylcholanthrene into its cancer-causing form, so tumors may have formed too quickly for any immune effect to show.⁠2

The revival began in the late 1990s, when mice that could not respond to IFN-γ, and mice lacking perforin (the pore-forming protein of killer cells, Chapter 5), turned out to develop more tumors.⁠3 The 2001 experiment closed the remaining loopholes. Knocking out RAG2 removes every T cell and B cell; knocking out the IFN-γ receptor or STAT1, a protein that relays its signal inside the cell, makes tissues unresponsive to IFN-γ. Both deficiencies raised tumor rates by similar amounts, and mice lacking both developed more spontaneous cancers of epithelial tissues (the linings of organs) as they aged.⁠3

The episode has a methodological lesson: an absent effect in a leaky model is not evidence of absence. It also broadened the concept. Burnet’s idea centered on lymphocytes, but innate cells such as NK cells and adaptive cells such as T cells both contribute, linked by IFN-γ.

The evidence from people#

Medicine has inadvertently run a version of that experiment in people. Transplant recipients take drugs for life that suppress their T cells. People living with HIV lose the CD4 helper T cells that coordinate immune responses (Chapter 5), although modern treatment now largely prevents that loss. If immunosurveillance is real, both groups should get more cancer, and they do.

Among about 176,000 US organ transplants, cancer overall was about twice as common as in the general population, but the rise was uneven. Kaposi sarcoma, a rare cancer driven by a herpesvirus, was about 60 times more common; non-Hodgkin lymphomas, often driven by the Epstein–Barr virus, about 7.5 times. Breast and prostate cancer were not increased.⁠4 These are ratios, not personal risks: in absolute terms, transplantation added roughly 7 extra cancers per 1,000 recipients per year.⁠4

This fits Part I: a virus-infected cell displays foreign peptides, and the same T cells probably also keep the viruses in check, so suppressing T cells makes virus-driven cancers far more common.⁠5

Virus-driven cancers still arise in people whose immune systems work, because these viruses are adapted to persist. Human papillomavirus lives in the lining of the cervix without killing cells or provoking inflammation; Epstein–Barr virus stays dormant for life inside resting B cells.⁠30,31 Most people clear or contain these infections; in a small minority, cancer develops years or decades later, often from infected cells that have acquired further ways to avoid T cells (Chapter 6).

Cancers that arise from mutated self proteins are harder for T cells to detect, so immunosuppression changes their risk less. Melanoma, for example, roughly doubles in transplant recipients but not in people with HIV, so the drugs (and closer skin checks) may explain the rise.⁠4,5 None of this means the immune system ignores breast or prostate cancer, only that any effect is too small to show in registry data. For those cancers, evidence of immune engagement comes from inside tumors; whether it prevents many of them is still debated (see the box at the end of the next section).

Go deeper Reading the human evidence carefully

Transplant recipients and people with HIV are imperfect stand-ins for “a body without immunosurveillance,” and the differences between them are informative.

First, some transplant drugs cause cancer directly. Azathioprine and cyclosporine are classified as human carcinogens, causing lymphoma and skin squamous-cell carcinoma. That skin cancer is about 46 times more common in transplant recipients but only about five times in people with HIV (in the one high-quality study available), which suggests that drugs and sunlight, not only weakened immunity, drive much of the transplant excess.⁠5

Second, the transplanted organ and the disease that made it necessary matter. Kidney cancer is raised most in kidney recipients, and most of the apparent excess of liver cancer is probably cancer that was already present in the diseased liver removed at transplant.⁠4 Screening matters too: people are checked for cancer before a transplant, which lowers the recorded rates of breast and prostate cancer, and closer medical follow-up afterwards finds more melanomas and kidney and thyroid cancers.⁠4

Third, a 2024 meta-analysis that pooled 113 studies — 67 in transplant recipients and 46 in people with HIV — found eight cancers raised only in transplant recipients (including melanoma, colorectal, kidney, bladder and thyroid cancers) and none raised only in people with HIV. Since both groups have weakened immunity but only one takes these drugs, the drugs probably explain some of that excess.⁠5

Cleaner evidence comes from Epstein–Barr lymphomas after stem-cell transplants, while the new immune system is still rebuilding. In one long-term study, T cells grown to recognize the virus were infused as a precaution into 101 patients, and none developed these lymphomas; in 13 who already had one, the infusions cleared it in 11.⁠32

People born with faulty immune systems are another informative group: among 3,658 in a US registry, lymphomas were 8 to 10 times as common as expected, while lung, colon, breast and prostate cancer were not raised.⁠33 And restoring immunity partly reverses the effect. In 1984, transplant surgeons reported that lymphomas, mostly driven by Epstein–Barr virus, often shrank when they lowered the immunosuppressive drugs.⁠34 After effective HIV treatment arrived in the late 1990s, Kaposi sarcoma fell by about two-thirds and non-Hodgkin lymphoma by about 40% among people with HIV, while other cancers did not change.⁠35

Two findings survive this scrutiny: virtually every virus-driven cancer rises in both groups, and breast and prostate cancer rise in neither, even after four decades of follow-up.⁠5

Other evidence comes from inside tumors. In 2006, a team in Paris counted immune cells in large series of colorectal cancers removed at surgery. In that study, the number, type and location of T cells (in the tumor’s center and along its invasive margin, where it pushes into healthy tissue) together predicted survival better than the standard staging system, which grades a tumor by how far it has grown and spread.⁠6 Similar links between T cells and survival have since been found in many, though not all, cancers.⁠7

In the clinic

The Paris finding became a test called the Immunoscore: a digital count of T cells (all T cells, and killer T cells) in a colon tumor’s center and at its margin. An international study enrolled 2,681 patients with stage I–III colon cancer (cancer that had not spread to distant organs). Among the first 700 analyzed, 8% of those with a high score had their cancer return within five years, compared with 32% of those with a low score. Two further groups of patients confirmed the pattern, and it held after accounting for stage and other known risk factors.⁠8

Key idea

The immune system acts against cancer. Mice and people with weakened immunity develop more cancers, especially virus-driven ones, and in many cancers, patients whose tumors contain many T cells tend to live longer.

Selection under immune pressure#

That leaves the question of why millions of people with working immune systems still develop cancer every year.

Part of the answer lies in the immune system’s own safeguards. Every mechanism that keeps T cells off healthy tissue — negative selection in the thymus, the requirement for signal 2, the brakes, regulatory T cells (Chapters 4 and 5) — also slows an attack on a cancer made of your own cells. Tolerance and tumor immunity pull against each other: releasing the brakes harms healthy tissue too (Chapter 8). The rest of the answer is the tumor’s own evolution.

The 2001 experiments also showed this evolution at work. The mice were inbred and genetically identical, so ordinary transplant rejection was ruled out. Even so, when the researchers moved tumors that had grown in immunodeficient mice into normal mice, about 40% were rejected; none of the tumors that had grown in normal mice were.⁠3 Tumors that grew without immune pressure were easy to recognize, while tumors that grew under it had already been selected to survive it.

Schreiber and his colleagues called this process cancer immunoediting, with three phases now known as the “three Es.”⁠2

  • Elimination. The immune system detects and destroys newly transformed cells. When it succeeds completely, the cancer is never detected.
  • Equilibrium. Some cancer cells survive but cannot expand: the immune system kills them, or holds back their growth, about as fast as new ones appear. This phase may last for years, perhaps the sixteen years in the kidney donor.
  • Escape. Eventually, a variant appears that the immune system can no longer control, and it grows into a visible tumor.

In 2007, the same group demonstrated equilibrium directly. Some mice given a small dose of carcinogen looked healthy but carried small, stable masses of cancer cells. When the researchers removed the animals’ T cells or blocked IFN-γ, many of those masses grew into tumors.⁠9

Immunoediting is natural selection under immune pressure. A tumor is a varied population of cells (Chapter 6); the immune system removes those that display the most recognizable neoantigens, so over years the population that reaches a doctor is selected for low visibility.

Selection of this kind has been documented in a mouse tumor, in which a single mutation created a strongly recognized neoantigen; T cells eliminated the cells carrying it, and the tumor that grew out came from cells that never had it.⁠10

Nothing in this is planned: tumor cells do not decide to hide, and they do not learn. Random mutation creates variants, the immune system removes some, and the survivors multiply. Immune cells can also end up helping the tumor (see “Recruit suppressor cells” below).

Go deeper Immunoediting observed directly

The mouse tumor described above was a chemically induced sarcoma, a cancer of connective tissue. The mutation that made it visible was in a protein called spectrin-β2, and the tumor that escaped grew from pre-existing clones that had never carried it: selection, not adaptation.⁠10

The 2007 equilibrium experiments added a complication. Cells in the stable masses divided slowly and were still highly visible, or “unedited”; only when a tumor broke out were its cells edited, less able to provoke rejection.⁠9 Removing NK cells did not release the masses, so in that model equilibrium depended on T cells and IFN-γ. (The simulation above simplifies this: in it, editing happens gradually during equilibrium.)

Human tumors show similar evidence of selection: the HLA losses described under “Hide” below. They are often present in only part of a tumor and are more common in metastases, the pattern expected of a late escape under immune selection rather than an early accident.⁠11

Go deeper How much cancer does immunosurveillance prevent?

That the immune system shapes tumors is well established. How many cancers it prevents in people is less clear, and skeptics have a serious case.

The case for caution. The cancers that rise when human immunity fails are mostly virus-driven. An early tumor deep in a tissue gives T cells little to respond to: naive T cells circulate only through blood and lymph nodes, the tumor produces few danger signals, and tumor antigens presented in a node without signal 2 tend to make T cells anergic, or unresponsive (Chapters 4 and 5). In mice in which a single random cell turns cancerous (closer to real life than an injected carcinogen), tumors with a strong foreign antigen were neither destroyed nor edited; instead they made their T cells tolerant.⁠36 In another model, T cells were already failing before the growth turned cancerous.⁠37 Searches of untreated human tumors for the loss of neoantigens that editing should cause have given mixed results.⁠38

The case for a larger role. Human tumors show signs of selection. About one in four carries a genetic change that helps it evade T cells, such as losing an HLA gene, and such changes are favored as tumors evolve.⁠39 In human airways, precancerous patches that later disappeared contained more immune cells than those that became cancer, and those that progressed had often acquired defects in antigen presentation.⁠40 In one study of digestive-tract cancers, most tumors examined (62 of 75) contained T cells recognizing their neoantigens (Chapter 6).⁠41 And in a 2025 mouse study of sarcomas started in leg muscle with fluorescently tagged neoantigens, T cells stopped about half from emerging, and every one that emerged had silenced its neoantigens.⁠42

Where the evidence stands. Immunosurveillance is real but uneven, and strongest against virus-driven cancers. Against many common cancers it may do less to stop tumors from starting than to shape the ones that grow. Elimination leaves no trace, so in people it can only be inferred, and its reach is unknown. Most tumors do appear to be recognized, though, and the T cells that recognize them are the starting material for much of Part III.

The cancer-immunity cycle#

Escape is easiest to describe against what a successful anti-tumor response requires, which two researchers set out in 2013 as a seven-step cancer-immunity cycle.⁠12

  1. Release. Cancer cells die and spill their proteins, including neoantigens.
  2. Presentation. Dendritic cells in the tumor take up this debris (Chapter 4). If danger signals are present, they mature and carry the antigens to a nearby lymph node; without danger signals, they induce tolerance instead.
  3. Priming. In the lymph node, the rare T cells with matching receptors receive signals 1 and 2 and multiply into a large clone of effector cells (Chapters 4 and 5).
  4. Trafficking. The new killer T cells circulate throughout the body but leave the blood only where a vessel wall displays the right adhesion molecules (selectins and integrin ligands) and chemokines, as vessels in inflamed tissue do (Chapters 2 and 4).
  5. Infiltration. They cross the vessel wall, a step called extravasation, and follow chemokine gradients through the tissue toward the cancer cells.
  6. Recognition. Each T cell checks the peptides on cancer cells’ MHC class I molecules for its one target.
  7. Killing. It kills matching cells with perforin and granzymes (Chapter 5).

These are the steps of the antiviral response at the end of Chapter 5, aimed at a tumor. The main difference is at the start.

With no microbe involved, danger signals must come from the tumor itself. Tumors are often inflamed, but mostly in the wound-healing way that dampens immune attack (Chapter 2); the kind of signal that lets dendritic cells prime killer T cells is rarer but does occur. In mice, DNA from dying cancer cells reaches a DNA-sensing pathway inside dendritic cells (named after its key protein, STING), which triggers type I interferon; without this pathway, the T-cell responses that tumors often provoke on their own largely fail.⁠13

Killing restarts the cycle. Cells killed by T cells die by apoptosis, which is fairly quiet (Chapter 5), but T cells also secrete IFN-γ and other cytokines that help keep nearby dendritic cells active. The debris can then be presented, recruiting T cells against new targets, a process called antigen spreading.⁠12

The steps work in series: a strong T-cell response achieves nothing if the cells cannot enter the tumor, and a tumor full of T cells is safe if it displays nothing they recognize. The weakest step limits the whole response.

Each immunotherapy in Part III acts at one or more of these steps. Some repair a step: among the checkpoint inhibitors, anti-CTLA-4 acts mainly at priming. Anti-PD-1 acts mainly at the last steps, freeing T cells in the tumor to recognize and kill, but it also replenishes the response from a reserve of stem-like T cells in lymph nodes and blood, feeding new rounds of priming and expansion (Chapters 5 and 8).⁠14 Vaccines boost presentation and priming (Chapter 11).

Other treatments make steps unnecessary. Engineered T cells and T-cell engagers supply ready-made killer cells, skipping the first three steps; CAR-T cells and most engagers also replace step 6, recognizing a surface protein without needing MHC class I. But all of them must still reach the tumor, get in and kill (Chapters 9 and 10).

Go deeper The cycle, revised

The wheel is a simplification, and its authors have revised it. In 2017 they added the idea of a cancer-immune set point: the amount of stimulation a particular tumor needs before an effective response begins. A tumor with many neoantigens that the person’s HLA types can display starts close to that threshold; one with few neoantigens, or exposed to high levels of TGF-β, starts far below it. The set point is shaped by the tumor’s genes, the person’s own genes and environmental factors such as medications and gut microbes.⁠15

In 2023, the cycle itself was updated in three main ways.⁠16

  • Part of the cycle runs inside the tumor. Dendritic cells in tumors keep re-stimulating T cells there, in local “niches”, rather than only through the lymph nodes. Patients whose tumors lack these niches tend to fare worse.
  • Checkpoint blockade works partly upstream. Much of its effect comes from the stem-like T-cell reserve described in Chapters 5 and 8, including cells in lymph nodes, which is why this chapter’s wheel marks anti-PD-1 at priming as well as at killing.
  • Context matters. The authors argue that a tumor’s immune state, which they call its “immunotype”, should be read alongside its cancer type and its mutations when choosing treatment.

A therapy still helps only if it fixes the step that is failing.

Key idea

An anti-tumor immune response is a cycle of seven steps, and the weakest step limits the whole response. Each kind of immunotherapy repairs particular steps or makes some unnecessary.

How tumors escape#

A tumor escapes when one step of the cycle fails and stays failed. The six main escape routes, described in turn below, are to hide from T cells, engage their brakes, recruit suppressor cells, build barriers, release suppressive molecules and ignore interferon.

Some act inside the cancer cell. Others reshape the tumor microenvironment: everything in a tumor that is not a cancer cell, from blood vessels and connective tissue to immune cells and the fibers between them.

Hide#

The most direct route is to stop displaying antigen. Every MHC class I molecule needs a small protein subunit, beta-2-microglobulin (B2M), to reach the cell surface, so a cancer cell that loses both working copies of the B2M gene displays no class I at all.

There are subtler versions. A tumor can lose one of the two inherited copies of an HLA gene (HLA loss of heterozygosity), deleting HLA types and, with them, every neoantigen that only those types could display. This happens in about 40% of non-small-cell lung cancers, the most common type of lung cancer,⁠11 and in about one tumor in six across all cancer types.⁠43 Or the tumor can lose the cells carrying the most visible neoantigens, as in the mouse tumor described earlier.

Hiding has a cost: a cell without MHC class I, especially a stressed one, invites attack by NK cells (missing self, Chapter 2), so a tumor that hides from T cells needs other routes as well.

Breaks step 6, recognition.

Brake#

One of the T-cell brakes from Chapter 5 is the inhibitory receptor PD-1, which dampens a T cell when it binds its ligand, PD-L1. Many tumors express PD-L1, often as a direct result of T-cell attack.

When T cells recognize a target, they secrete IFN-γ, which among other effects makes nearby cells express more PD-L1, a response that normally protects healthy tissue from excessive inflammation. In a study of melanomas and other pigment-cell growths, T cells were nearby in 98% of samples with PD-L1 but only 28% of those without, and PD-L1 was found where T cells and IFN-γ were.⁠17

This is called adaptive resistance; “adaptive” refers to the tumor adapting to the attack, not to adaptive immunity. It also means that a tumor using this brake has already been found by the immune system; releasing the brake is what the drugs in Chapter 8 do.

Breaks step 7, killing.

Recruit suppressor cells#

Tumors accumulate immune cells that suppress rather than attack.⁠18

  • Regulatory T cells (Tregs), whose normal job is to prevent autoimmunity (Chapter 5), are drawn into many tumors, where they inhibit killer T cells.
  • Myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) are immature relatives of neutrophils and monocytes (the blood-borne precursors of macrophages). The bone marrow releases them in large numbers during cancer, and they disable T cells, for example by depleting arginine, an amino acid T cells need, and by producing reactive oxygen species, unstable oxygen-containing molecules that damage them.⁠19
  • Tumor-associated macrophages often shift toward the wound-healing, “M2-like” state described in Chapter 2. They clear debris, promote blood-vessel growth and secrete anti-inflammatory cytokines, which help repair a wound but also help a tumor grow. (Real tumor macrophages usually mix M1 and M2 features.)

Macrophages and related cells make up a large share of the immune cells in many solid tumors, and their effects go both ways: they can also present antigen and, guided by antibody drugs, engulf cancer cells (Chapter 9).

Weakens step 7, killing, and step 3, priming.

Build barriers#

Around many tumors, cancer-associated fibroblasts, connective-tissue cells recruited and reprogrammed by the tumor, deposit dense collagen, the main structural protein of connective tissue. Together with abnormal blood vessels, this fibrous tissue, called stroma, forms a barrier that T cells struggle to cross.

In a large study of advanced bladder (urothelial) cancer treated with a PD-L1-blocking drug, many non-responders had killer T cells trapped in the stroma, along with signs of TGF-β, a cytokine that drives scarring (fibrosis), acting on the fibroblasts. In mice with similar tumors, blocking TGF-β along with PD-L1 let T cells enter the tumor’s center, and the tumors shrank.⁠20 This has proved hard to translate into people. When a drug that blocks both TGF-β and PD-L1 was compared with a standard PD-1 blocker in a large lung-cancer trial, it did worse: the cancer progressed sooner on average (though the gap could have been chance), severe side effects were three times as common, and the trial was stopped early.⁠21

Not every barrier is made of collagen. A tumor’s vessels can lack the adhesion molecules T cells need to stop, and some express Fas ligand, which triggers apoptosis in cells carrying its receptor, Fas (Chapter 5); it kills arriving killer T cells but spares Tregs.⁠44 Some tumors silence chemokines, such as CXCL9 and CXCL10, that help draw T cells in and keep them active.⁠45 Inside the tumor, T cells migrate along collagen fibers, and fibers that encircle a nest of cancer cells steer them around it.⁠46 Often the problem is access: the right T cells exist but cannot get in.

Breaks steps 4 and 5, trafficking and infiltration.

Release suppressive molecules#

Conditions inside a tumor impair T cells. Cells there use up nutrients, outgrow their blood supply (leaving pockets short of oxygen, a state called hypoxia) and secrete lactic acid, and T cells function poorly in acidic, oxygen-poor tissue. Tumors also produce molecules that inhibit T cells or starve them of nutrients, such as adenosine and an enzyme called IDO (see the box). Together with TGF-β, these weaken even the T cells that get inside.⁠22

Weakens step 7, killing.

Go deeper Suppressive molecules in detail

Each of these is a normal physiological signal, produced in excess and for too long.⁠22

Adenosine. Outside cells, ATP, the cell’s main energy carrier, acts as a danger signal. Two enzymes on cell surfaces, CD39 and CD73, convert it step by step into adenosine, which binds the A2A receptor on T cells and raises cyclic AMP, a small signaling molecule inside the cell that inhibits T-cell activity. Hypoxia increases the expression of both enzymes. Drugs that block CD73 or the A2A receptor are in clinical trials.

IDO and kynurenine. Indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase 1 (IDO1) converts the amino acid tryptophan into kynurenine. T cells sense the missing tryptophan as starvation, and kynurenine pushes them toward regulatory behavior. Despite strong laboratory data, an IDO1 inhibitor added to anti-PD-1 failed in a large melanoma trial (Chapter 8).⁠23

Glucose, lactate and acidity. Many cancer cells break down glucose rapidly even when oxygen is available, and secrete lactic acid; the acidic, lactate-rich fluid reduces T cells’ production of IFN-γ. Whether T cells lose a competition for glucose is debated. When researchers traced nutrient uptake inside mouse tumors, immune cells, especially macrophages and related myeloid cells, took up more glucose than the cancer cells did; the cancer cells preferred glutamine, and glucose was not the limiting nutrient. Nutrient use seems to be set more by each cell type’s own programming than by competition.⁠24

Prostaglandin E2. The signaling molecule behind fever and inflammatory pain (Chapter 2) is made in large amounts by many tumors. In mice it weakens NK cells and so deprives the tumor of the cross-presenting dendritic cells those NK cells recruit (Chapter 4); blocking its production with aspirin-like drugs helped anti-PD-1 clear tumors.⁠47

Hypoxia. Low oxygen activates HIF-1α, a transcription factor (a protein that controls which genes are expressed), which reshapes cell metabolism, raises adenosine production and favors suppressive myeloid cells.

TGF-β. Besides promoting fibrous barriers, TGF-β directly suppresses killer T cells and NK cells and can convert some ordinary CD4 T cells into Tregs, though most Tregs in human tumors seem to arrive already formed.⁠48

Because these signals overlap, blocking any single one has rarely been enough on its own.

Ignore interferon#

Finally, tumors can stop responding to IFN-γ. Besides raising PD-L1, IFN-γ from T cells makes cancer cells express more MHC and can halt their growth. Its signal is relayed inside the cell by enzymes called JAK1 and JAK2.

Researchers sequenced the regrown tumors of four melanoma patients whose cancer had first shrunk on a PD-1-blocking drug. Three of the four had a clear explanation: two had lost working JAK1 or JAK2 and no longer responded to IFN-γ, and one had lost B2M and, with it, all MHC class I display.⁠25 Chapter 12 returns to these resistance mechanisms.

Breaks steps 6 and 7.

None of these defenses is planned; each spread because the cells that happened to have it survived. The non-cancer cells involved are mostly doing their ordinary jobs: Tregs, M2-like macrophages and fibroblasts calm inflammation and repair tissue, and to the body a tumor can look like a wound that needs healing.

Inflamed, excluded and desert tumors#

Under a microscope, a slice of tumor can reveal its immune history. Researchers sort tumors into three broad immune profiles.⁠15

  • Inflamed: T cells are inside the tumor, among the cancer cells, often alongside PD-L1 and other brakes. The cycle ran, but the last step was blocked.
  • Excluded: T cells arrived but are stuck at the edges, in the stroma, held back by the barriers described above.
  • Desert: few T cells anywhere. The cycle barely started, because of too little antigen, no activated dendritic cells or no priming. Sometimes the tumor’s own driver mutations are responsible (see the box on driver genes).

In the news and in many papers, inflamed tumors are called “hot” and desert tumors “cold”, though “cold” is often stretched to cover excluded tumors too.

Real tumors do not fall neatly into three categories, but the profiles help predict who will benefit from immunotherapy. Checkpoint inhibitors release brakes on T cells that already recognize the tumor, mostly those inside it plus a reserve in the lymph nodes, so responses occur most often in inflamed tumors, though not every inflamed tumor responds. Releasing the brakes does little for T cells held outside a barrier, or for T cells that don’t exist.⁠15 Tumors rich in B cells, especially when organized into miniature lymph nodes (see the box on tertiary lymphoid structures), also tend to respond better.⁠26

That is why much of Part III, and especially Chapter 12, is about bringing T cells into excluded and desert tumors.

Go deeper Driver genes that shape the immune profile

Some resistance comes from the same mutations that make a cell cancerous (Chapter 6). Three examples:

  • WNT/β-catenin. In some melanomas, an overactive WNT/β-catenin pathway suppresses the chemokines that would recruit a key type of dendritic cell. Without those dendritic cells, T cells are never primed against the tumor, and it remains a desert.⁠27
  • PTEN loss. PTEN is a tumor suppressor that restrains a growth pathway called PI3K–AKT. In melanoma models, cancer cells that lose it secrete more suppressive cytokines, let in fewer T cells and resist being killed. In patients, PTEN loss went with fewer T cells in the tumor and poorer responses to anti-PD-1. In mice, blocking the pathway PTEN normally restrains made checkpoint inhibitors work better.⁠28
  • STK11 (also called LKB1). In lung adenocarcinomas driven by a mutated KRAS gene, a second mutation in STK11 marks tumors that rarely respond to PD-1 blockade: in one group of 174 patients, about 7% of these tumors responded, against 29–36% of other KRAS-driven tumors. Such tumors often lack PD-L1 even when they carry many mutations.⁠29

This connects the two kinds of brake described in Chapter 6: the same mutations that drive a tumor’s growth can also shape its immune profile.

Go deeper Tertiary lymphoid structures: a lymph node inside the tumor

In some tumors, immune cells organize themselves into miniature lymph nodes, called tertiary lymphoid structures (TLS). Like a lymph node, a mature TLS has a core of B cells surrounded by T cells and dendritic cells, supplied by the same entry vessels lymph nodes use, high endothelial venules (Chapter 4). TLS form at sites of long-lasting inflammation, usually at a tumor’s edge or in its stroma.⁠26 In mice, such vessels were the main route by which T cells entered tumors, with or without checkpoint therapy, and in 93 people with metastatic melanoma, tumors that had them responded better to anti-PD-1 plus anti-CTLA-4.⁠49

In 2020, three studies in melanoma, kidney cancer and soft-tissue sarcoma found that tumors rich in B cells and TLS were more likely to respond to checkpoint inhibitors, and their patients tended to live longer. Similar associations have since been reported across many cancer types.⁠26

A TLS might help by shortening the cancer-immunity cycle, letting presentation and priming happen next to the tumor instead of in a distant lymph node. B cells inside them may also produce antibodies against the tumor and present antigens to T cells.

As with any such association, TLS may cause better responses or may only mark tumors that were already well recognized. Researchers are testing whether they can be induced deliberately; this is still experimental.

Key idea

Where the T cells sit — inside the tumor, stuck at its edges, or nowhere — helps predict whether immunotherapy will work.

From escape routes to treatments#

The kidney donor’s melanoma cells were in equilibrium: alive but held in check. In the recipients, immunosuppressive drugs removed that control, and the cells escaped. In the man who survived, doctors let his immune defenses return and removed the tumor-bearing kidney.

Modern immunotherapy works on the same principle. Rather than only removing or destroying cancer with surgery, radiation or chemotherapy, it acts on the immune response: releasing brakes, breaking down barriers, making hidden cancer cells visible again, or supplying new T cells. Each escape route is also a potential target.

The immune system probably held one melanoma in check for sixteen years, yet medicine took more than a century to learn how to strengthen that kind of control; the history that follows explains why. Chapter 8 then turns to the drugs that first made the approach work for many patients: checkpoint inhibitors.

Before you go

Takeaways

  • Mice lacking lymphocytes or IFN-γ signaling develop more tumors, and people with weakened immunity develop more cancers, above all virus-driven ones. In many cancers, more T cells in the tumor go with longer survival.
  • Immunoediting has three phases: elimination, equilibrium and escape. Immune pressure selects for less visible cancer cells, so the tumors that reach a doctor have already been selected to survive it.
  • An effective response is a seven-step cancer-immunity cycle: release, presentation, priming, trafficking, infiltration, recognition, killing. Breaking any step stalls it. Treatments repair steps or make some unnecessary, but T cells must always still travel, get in and kill.
  • Tumors escape by hiding, engaging T-cell brakes, recruiting suppressor cells, building barriers, releasing suppressive molecules and ignoring interferon, mostly by exploiting normal programs of tolerance and wound healing.
  • PD-L1 next to T cells is often a sign of an immune attack that has been braked, the situation checkpoint inhibitors are designed for.
  • Inflamed, excluded and desert tumors differ in where the T cells are, and that difference strongly shapes who benefits from immunotherapy.
