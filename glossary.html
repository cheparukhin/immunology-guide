A

Accelerated approval
A US FDA pathway that approves a drug on early evidence, such as tumor shrinkage, on condition that later trials confirm a clinical benefit to patients. If they do not, the approval can be withdrawn.
Introduced in Interlude · 130 Years of Immunotherapy
Acquired resistance
When a cancer that first responded to a treatment later grows back despite it.
Introduced in Chapter 12 · Resistance and New Directions
Adaptive immunity
The slower, highly specific branch of the immune system, made up of T cells, B cells and the antibodies B cells secrete. It generates millions of different receptors in advance, expands the cells whose receptors bind a particular antigen (clonal expansion), and keeps memory cells that recognize that antigen for years.
Introduced in Chapter 1 · Cells, Proteins and Molecular Recognition
Adaptive resistance
A tumor’s response to immune attack, in which signals from T cells (especially IFN-γ) cause the tumor to raise brakes such as PD-L1. “Adaptive” refers to the tumor adapting, not to adaptive immunity.
Introduced in Chapter 7 · Immune Surveillance and Tumor Escape
ADCC (antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity)
Killing of an antibody-coated cell by an immune cell, usually a natural killer (NK) cell, that binds the antibodies’ Fc regions with its Fc receptors.
Introduced in Chapter 3 · Adaptive Immunity
ADCP (antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis)
Engulfment of an antibody-coated cell by a macrophage or other phagocyte that binds the antibodies’ Fc regions with its Fc receptors.
Introduced in Chapter 9 · Therapeutic Antibodies
Adenosine
A small molecule produced outside cells from ATP; in tumors it binds receptors on T cells and suppresses their activity.
Introduced in Chapter 7 · Immune Surveillance and Tumor Escape
Adhesion molecule
A surface protein that binds a ligand on another cell or in the surrounding tissue, holding cells together or in place. Selectins, integrins and the ligands they bind are the adhesion molecules that let white blood cells leave the blood.
Introduced in Chapter 2 · Innate Immunity
Adjuvant
An ingredient added to a vaccine that activates innate danger sensors, such as pattern-recognition receptors, so that antigen-presenting cells mature and the adaptive response to the vaccine’s antigen is strong. Not to be confused with adjuvant therapy, which is treatment after surgery.
Introduced in Chapter 2 · Innate Immunity
Adjuvant therapy
Treatment given after surgery to destroy any undetected cancer cells left behind and lower the risk of relapse. Not to be confused with a vaccine adjuvant.
Introduced in Chapter 8 · Checkpoint Inhibitors
Affinity
The strength of binding between two molecules at a single binding site, usually measured as a dissociation constant (KD). It reflects the balance between binding and dissociation; for most immune molecules, mainly how long partners stay bound.
Introduced in Chapter 1 · Cells, Proteins and Molecular Recognition
Affinity maturation
The gradual improvement in how tightly antibodies bind their target during an immune response, through rounds of mutation and selection.
Introduced in Chapter 3 · Adaptive Immunity
AIRE (autoimmune regulator)
A protein in specialized epithelial cells of the thymic medulla that activates genes otherwise expressed only in other organs, so developing T cells can be tested against those proteins.
Introduced in Chapter 5 · T Cells: Selection, Killing and Regulation
Allele
One of the different versions of a gene that exist across a population. More than 45,000 HLA alleles were known by mid-2026.
Introduced in Chapter 4 · Antigen Presentation
Allogeneic
Coming from a donor rather than the patient. Allogeneic (“off-the-shelf”) products could be made in batches in advance, but risk attacking the recipient or being rejected by them.
Introduced in Chapter 10 · Cell Therapies
Amino acid
One of 20 small molecules that link into chains to make proteins. They differ in size, in charge and in how hydrophobic they are, which governs how a chain folds.
Introduced in Chapter 1 · Cells, Proteins and Molecular Recognition
Anchor residue
One of the few amino acids in a peptide (for MHC class I, often the second and the last) that must fit pockets in the floor of the peptide-binding groove for the peptide to stay bound. Different HLA versions have differently shaped pockets.
Introduced in Chapter 4 · Antigen Presentation
Anergy
A lasting unresponsive state that a T cell enters when it recognizes its target without receiving the confirming (costimulatory) signal. The cell stays alive but no longer responds; this protects against autoimmunity but can also silence T cells able to attack a tumor.
Introduced in Chapter 4 · Antigen Presentation
Anti-drug antibodies
Antibodies a patient makes against a protein drug. They can neutralize the drug, speed its removal or cause allergic reactions.
Introduced in Chapter 9 · Therapeutic Antibodies
Antibody
A Y-shaped protein secreted by B cells; it is the secreted form of the B-cell receptor. Its two identical arms (Fab arms) bind one specific molecular feature, the epitope, and its stem (the Fc region) is bound by receptors on immune cells and by complement proteins. An antibody can neutralize its target or mark it for destruction.
Introduced in Chapter 1 · Cells, Proteins and Molecular Recognition
Antibody class (isotype)
One of five antibody types (IgG, IgA, IgM, IgE, IgD), set by the heavy chain’s constant region. Classes share the same kind of variable regions but have different Fc regions and therefore different jobs.
Introduced in Chapter 3 · Adaptive Immunity
Antibody–drug conjugate (ADC)
An antibody joined by a chemical linker to a potent cytotoxic (cell-killing) drug, the payload. The antibody delivers the payload to cells that express its target; these usually internalize the conjugate and release the drug inside.
Introduced in Interlude · 130 Years of Immunotherapy
Antigen
Anything that the immune system’s specific receptors (on T cells, on B cells, or antibodies) can recognize, such as part of a virus, a toxin or a protein from a cancer cell. It is often a protein or a fragment of one.
Introduced in Chapter 1 · Cells, Proteins and Molecular Recognition
Antigen escape
Relapse of a cancer whose cells no longer carry a therapy’s target in a form the therapy recognizes, because treatment selected for cells that lacked the target, lost it or changed it.
Introduced in Chapter 9 · Therapeutic Antibodies
Antigen presentation
The process by which a cell degrades proteins into short peptides and displays them on its surface in MHC molecules, so that T cells can detect what the cell is making or has taken up.
Introduced in Chapter 4 · Antigen Presentation
Antigen spreading
The broadening of an immune response to new targets, as killing releases additional antigens that activate new T cells.
Introduced in Chapter 7 · Immune Surveillance and Tumor Escape
Apoptosis
Programmed cell death, in which a cell dismantles itself into membrane-wrapped fragments that phagocytes engulf, without spilling its contents or causing inflammation.
Introduced in Chapter 2 · Innate Immunity
Armored CAR
A CAR-T cell carrying an extra gene, usually for a cytokine, that it releases on engaging its target, intended to help immune cells survive and work inside a suppressive tumor. Experimental.
Introduced in Chapter 10 · Cell Therapies
Autoimmunity
An immune attack on the body’s own healthy tissues, caused by a failure of tolerance, as in type 1 diabetes, multiple sclerosis or rheumatoid arthritis.
Introduced in Chapter 1 · Cells, Proteins and Molecular Recognition
Autologous
Coming from the patient’s own body. Every approved CAR-T, TIL and TCR-T product is autologous.
Introduced in Chapter 10 · Cell Therapies
Avidity
The overall binding strength of a molecule that binds its target at several sites at once, such as a two-armed antibody. It can be far greater than the affinity of any single site.
Introduced in Chapter 1 · Cells, Proteins and Molecular Recognition

B

B cell
A lymphocyte that carries one kind of antibody on its surface as its receptor and, when activated, can differentiate into a plasma cell that secretes that antibody. B cells mature in the bone marrow; the “B” comes from the bursa of Fabricius, the bird organ where they were discovered.
Introduced in Chapter 1 · Cells, Proteins and Molecular Recognition
B-cell receptor (BCR)
An antibody anchored in a B cell’s membrane; each B cell carries many copies of one kind.
Introduced in Chapter 3 · Adaptive Immunity
B7 (CD80 and CD86)
Two related proteins on dendritic cells and other antigen-presenting cells, whose expression rises sharply when danger signals make those cells mature. They deliver costimulation (signal 2) by binding the activating receptor CD28 on T cells, but the inhibitory receptor CTLA-4 binds them with higher affinity and competes with CD28 for them.
Introduced in Chapter 4 · Antigen Presentation
BCG (bacillus Calmette–Guérin)
A weakened relative of the tuberculosis bacterium, used as a TB vaccine and, instilled into the bladder, as a treatment for early bladder cancer.
Introduced in Interlude · 130 Years of Immunotherapy
BCMA (B-cell maturation antigen)
A surface protein on antibody-producing plasma cells and on the cancerous plasma cells of multiple myeloma. It is the target of several CAR-T therapies and T-cell engagers for myeloma.
Introduced in Chapter 9 · Therapeutic Antibodies
Benign
Describes a tumor that grows but stays within its boundaries, without invading nearby tissue or spreading.
Introduced in Chapter 6 · Cancer Cells and Tumor Antigens
Beta-2-microglobulin (B2M)
A small protein subunit of every MHC class I molecule; without it, class I molecules cannot fold properly or reach the cell surface. Cells that lose B2M cannot present peptides to killer T cells.
Introduced in Chapter 7 · Immune Surveillance and Tumor Escape
Biomarker
A measurable feature, such as a protein, a gene change or a blood level, that indicates something about a disease or predicts how it will behave or respond to treatment.
Introduced in Chapter 6 · Cancer Cells and Tumor Antigens
Biopsy
A small sample of tissue removed for examination, for example to confirm a cancer or to see which immune cells are inside it.
Introduced in Chapter 7 · Immune Surveillance and Tumor Escape
Biosimilar
A near-identical, usually cheaper, copy of an antibody or other biological drug, sold after the original’s patents expire.
Introduced in Chapter 12 · Resistance and New Directions
Bispecific antibody
An engineered antibody with two different antigen-binding sites, so it can bind two different targets at once, for example a protein on a tumor cell and CD3 on a T cell.
Introduced in Interlude · 130 Years of Immunotherapy
Blood cancer
A cancer of blood-forming or immune cells (leukemia, lymphoma or myeloma), growing mainly in the bone marrow, blood and lymph nodes.
Introduced in Chapter 6 · Cancer Cells and Tumor Antigens
Bone marrow
The soft tissue inside bones where blood cells are made, including the ancestors of all immune cells. B cells mature there, and it holds about 40% of the body’s immune cells.
Introduced in Chapter 1 · Cells, Proteins and Molecular Recognition
Bystander effect
Killing of neighboring cells by an ADC payload that leaks out of the targeted cell, which can reach cancer cells lacking the target.
Introduced in Chapter 9 · Therapeutic Antibodies

C

Cancer
A disease in which the body’s own cells, altered by accumulated mutations, multiply out of control and can invade other tissues.
Introduced in Chapter 1 · Cells, Proteins and Molecular Recognition
Cancer immunoediting
The process by which the immune system both destroys cancer cells and, by removing the most visible ones, shapes the tumor that eventually escapes. Its phases are elimination, equilibrium and escape.
Introduced in Chapter 7 · Immune Surveillance and Tumor Escape
Cancer-associated fibroblast (CAF)
A connective-tissue cell recruited and reprogrammed by a tumor; CAFs deposit dense collagen and secrete signals that can keep T cells out.
Introduced in Chapter 7 · Immune Surveillance and Tumor Escape
Cancer-immunity cycle
A framework (Chen and Mellman, 2013) describing the seven steps of an immune attack on a tumor: antigen release, presentation by dendritic cells, priming in lymph nodes, trafficking, infiltration, recognition and killing. Killing releases more antigen, which can start the cycle again.
Introduced in Chapter 7 · Immune Surveillance and Tumor Escape
Cancer-testis antigen
A protein made almost nowhere in a healthy adult except in developing sperm cells — which carry no MHC class I and so never show it to T cells — but re-expressed in many cancers. Examples include NY-ESO-1 and MAGE-A4.
Introduced in Chapter 6 · Cancer Cells and Tumor Antigens
CAR-NK cell
A natural killer cell given a chimeric antigen receptor. Early studies suggest less cytokine release syndrome and less risk from donor cells, at the cost of persisting for a shorter time.
Introduced in Chapter 10 · Cell Therapies
CAR-T cell
A T cell, usually the patient’s own, genetically engineered to express a chimeric antigen receptor (CAR), a synthetic receptor that joins an antibody’s antigen-binding variable regions to T-cell signaling domains. Because the cells expand in the patient and can persist for years, they are called a “living drug”.
Introduced in Interlude · 130 Years of Immunotherapy
Carcinogen
Any substance or exposure that can cause cancer, usually by damaging DNA — such as tobacco smoke, UV light or certain chemicals.
Introduced in Chapter 7 · Immune Surveillance and Tumor Escape
Carcinoma
A cancer that starts in epithelial cells, which line the skin and the surfaces of organs. Most common cancers, such as lung, breast, colon and prostate cancer, are carcinomas.
Introduced in Chapter 6 · Cancer Cells and Tumor Antigens
CD19
A protein on the surface of nearly every B cell, healthy or cancerous, including most B-cell leukemias and lymphomas. It is the target of blinatumomab and of the first successful CAR-T cells, chosen partly because people can live without B cells if given antibodies from donors.
Introduced in Chapter 9 · Therapeutic Antibodies
CD20
A protein on the surface of nearly all B cells, but not on the blood-forming cells that make them or on plasma cells. It is the target of rituximab and several engagers.
Introduced in Chapter 9 · Therapeutic Antibodies
CD28
The main costimulatory (activating) receptor on T cells, the counterpart of inhibitory receptors such as CTLA-4. When it binds B7 on a mature dendritic cell while the T-cell receptor recognizes its target, it delivers signal 2, which lets a naive T cell activate instead of becoming anergic.
Introduced in Chapter 4 · Antigen Presentation
CD3
The cluster of signaling chains bound to every T-cell receptor, together forming the TCR–CD3 complex. The receptor’s antigen-binding chains cannot signal on their own; CD3 relays the activating signal into the cell. T-cell engagers (Chapter 9) bind CD3 to activate T cells.
Introduced in Chapter 4 · Antigen Presentation
CD3ζ (CD3 zeta)
A signaling chain of the T-cell receptor’s CD3 complex that carries signal 1 into the cell when the receptor binds its target. Its intracellular domain is the activating part of every approved chimeric antigen receptor.
Introduced in Chapter 10 · Cell Therapies
CD40 and CD40 ligand
A receptor on dendritic cells, B cells and macrophages (CD40) and its partner on activated helper T cells (CD40 ligand). Their binding lets helpers “license” these cells.
Introduced in Chapter 5 · T Cells: Selection, Killing and Regulation
CD47
A “don’t eat me” signal on the surface of cells that inhibits their phagocytosis by macrophages. Many cancers overexpress it; red blood cells depend on it too.
Introduced in Chapter 2 · Innate Immunity
CDC (complement-dependent cytotoxicity)
Killing of an antibody-coated cell by complement: proteins activated by the antibodies’ clustered Fc regions assemble pores in the cell’s membrane.
Introduced in Chapter 9 · Therapeutic Antibodies
CDR3
The most variable loop of an antibody or T-cell receptor, created at the V(D)J junctions and sitting at the center of the antigen-binding site.
Introduced in Chapter 3 · Adaptive Immunity
Cell
The basic unit of life: a microscopic compartment wrapped in a membrane that uses energy, responds to its surroundings and, usually, can divide. An adult human has roughly 30 trillion.
Introduced in Chapter 1 · Cells, Proteins and Molecular Recognition
Cell membrane
The lipid bilayer that surrounds every cell, separating inside from outside. Proteins embedded in it act as channels, sensors and identity markers.
Introduced in Chapter 1 · Cells, Proteins and Molecular Recognition
Checkpoint inhibitor
A drug, usually a monoclonal antibody, that blocks an immune checkpoint such as PD-1, its ligand PD-L1, or CTLA-4, releasing a brake on T cells so they can keep attacking.
Introduced in Chapter 8 · Checkpoint Inhibitors
Chemokine
A type of cytokine that attracts cells. Immune cells migrate along its concentration gradient toward the cells secreting it.
Introduced in Chapter 2 · Innate Immunity
Chemotherapy
Drugs that kill rapidly dividing cells, usually by damaging DNA or blocking the process of cell division. They hit cancer cells but also harm healthy dividing cells, such as those in the bone marrow, gut lining and hair follicles.
Introduced in Chapter 8 · Checkpoint Inhibitors
Chimeric antibody
An engineered antibody in which a mouse antibody’s variable regions, which bind the target, are joined to human constant regions. Classic names contain -xi-, as in rituximab. The name refers to the Chimera, a creature of Greek myth made from parts of different animals.
Introduced in Chapter 9 · Therapeutic Antibodies
Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)
A laboratory-made receptor that combines an antibody’s antigen-binding variable regions (an scFv), outside the cell, with T-cell signaling domains (CD3ζ plus, in approved products, one costimulatory domain), inside it, joined by a hinge and a transmembrane domain. “Chimeric” because it is assembled from parts of different molecules, after the Chimera of Greek myth, a creature made from parts of different animals.
Introduced in Chapter 10 · Cell Therapies
Chromosome
One of the long DNA molecules, wrapped around packaging proteins, into which a genome is divided. Most human cells carry 46, half from each parent.
Introduced in Chapter 3 · Adaptive Immunity
Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA)
Fragments of DNA released into the blood by cancer cells, identifiable by the tumor’s own mutations.
Introduced in Chapter 12 · Resistance and New Directions
Class switching
A DNA change in an activated B cell that joins its variable-region gene to a different heavy-chain constant-region gene, changing the antibody’s class (for example from IgM to IgG) and its Fc region without changing what it binds.
Introduced in Chapter 3 · Adaptive Immunity
Clinical complete response
No sign of cancer on any examination, scan or biopsy after treatment, without surgery to confirm it under the microscope.
Introduced in Chapter 12 · Resistance and New Directions
Clinical trial
A study that tests a treatment in people, usually in phases: phase 1 tests safety and dose, phase 2 looks for early signs of benefit, and phase 3 compares the new treatment with the standard one in large, usually randomized groups.
Introduced in Interlude · 130 Years of Immunotherapy
Clonal (vs subclonal) neoantigen
A clonal neoantigen comes from a mutation present in every cancer cell, the trunk of the tumor’s family tree; a subclonal one is found only in some branches.
Introduced in Chapter 6 · Cancer Cells and Tumor Antigens
Clonal evolution
The process, set out for cancer by Peter Nowell in 1976, by which random mutation and selection let ever-fitter clones take over a tumor.
Introduced in Chapter 6 · Cancer Cells and Tumor Antigens
Clonal expansion
The rapid, repeated division of the rare lymphocytes whose receptors recognize a target, producing thousands to millions of identical cells (a clone).
Introduced in Chapter 3 · Adaptive Immunity
Clonal replacement
The observation that many T-cell clones expanding in a tumor after PD-1 blockade are new arrivals rather than clones that were there before treatment.
Introduced in Chapter 8 · Checkpoint Inhibitors
Clonal selection
The principle that an antigen activates only the pre-existing lymphocytes whose receptors already fit it, which then multiply.
Introduced in Chapter 3 · Adaptive Immunity
Clone
A family of cells descended from a single ancestor cell. In immunology, all the lymphocytes that share the same receptor (B-cell clones later diversify slightly by mutation).
Introduced in Chapter 3 · Adaptive Immunity
Co-receptor
CD8 or CD4: a molecule on a T cell that binds the same MHC molecule as the T-cell receptor, at a site away from the peptide-binding groove, stabilizing the contact and helping to start signaling. CD8 binds MHC class I and CD4 binds class II, which ties killer T cells to class I and helper T cells to class II.
Introduced in Chapter 4 · Antigen Presentation
Codon
A group of three bases in DNA or RNA that specifies one amino acid, or the start or end of a protein.
Introduced in Chapter 1 · Cells, Proteins and Molecular Recognition
Cold tumor
Informal term for a tumor with few or no T cells inside. Strictly a “desert”, though often also used for “excluded” tumors whose T cells are stuck at the edges.
Introduced in Chapter 7 · Immune Surveillance and Tumor Escape
Collagen
The body’s main structural protein: tough fibers that give skin, tendons and scar tissue their strength.
Introduced in Chapter 7 · Immune Surveillance and Tumor Escape
Complement
A set of dozens of blood proteins that, once triggered on a target’s surface (by a microbe, or by antibodies clustered on a cell), set off a cascade of reactions that opsonizes the target for phagocytes, attracts immune cells and can form pores in its membrane.
Introduced in Chapter 2 · Innate Immunity
Complementarity-determining regions (CDRs)
The six short loops at the end of each antibody arm, three from the heavy chain’s variable region and three from the light chain’s, that contact the target. They differ most from one antibody to the next; a humanized antibody keeps only the mouse CDRs.
Introduced in Chapter 9 · Therapeutic Antibodies
Constant region
The part of an antibody chain that comes in only a few standard versions and does not bind the target. The heavy chain’s constant region sets the antibody’s class (IgG, IgA and so on), and the lower parts of the two heavy chains’ constant regions form the Fc region.
Introduced in Chapter 3 · Adaptive Immunity
Corticosteroid
A drug related to the body’s stress hormone cortisol, such as prednisone, that strongly suppresses inflammation and immune activity.
Introduced in Chapter 8 · Checkpoint Inhibitors
Cortisol
The body’s main stress hormone, made by the adrenal glands on instructions from the pituitary. It is essential for life; if the glands are damaged, it must be replaced with pills.
Introduced in Chapter 8 · Checkpoint Inhibitors
Costimulation (signal 2)
The confirming second signal a T cell needs, in addition to recognizing its target (signal 1), to become fully activated: the second factor in two-factor authentication. It is classically delivered when CD28 on the T cell binds B7 on a mature dendritic cell; recognition without it can leave the T cell anergic (lastingly unresponsive).
Introduced in Chapter 4 · Antigen Presentation
Cross-presentation
The ability of certain dendritic cells to present peptides from material they have taken up from outside, for example from a dead tumor cell, on their own MHC class I molecules, so that killer T cells can be primed against it.
Introduced in Chapter 4 · Antigen Presentation
Cross-reactivity
The ability of a receptor or antibody to bind molecules that resemble its intended target, usually more weakly.
Introduced in Chapter 1 · Cells, Proteins and Molecular Recognition
CTLA-4
An inhibitory receptor (a “brake”) that T cells express after activation and regulatory T cells express constantly. A close relative of CD28, it binds the same B7 molecules with higher affinity, outcompetes CD28 for them and can remove them from antigen-presenting cells, so it acts mainly while T cells are being primed in lymph nodes; ipilimumab blocks it.
Introduced in Chapter 4 · Antigen Presentation
Cytokine
A small signaling protein that cells, especially immune cells, secrete to change the behavior of other cells, for example to make them divide, migrate, attack or suppress a response. Interferons and interleukins are examples.
Introduced in Chapter 2 · Innate Immunity
Cytokine release syndrome (CRS)
A body-wide inflammatory reaction, with fever, low blood pressure and sometimes low oxygen or organ damage, set off when a therapy such as CAR-T cells or a T-cell engager activates large numbers of immune cells at once. Much of the main cytokine involved, IL-6, is secreted by the patient’s own macrophages and related cells rather than by the T cells. Popularly called a “cytokine storm”.
Introduced in Chapter 2 · Innate Immunity

D

DAMP (damage-associated molecular pattern)
A molecule that normally stays inside cells, such as ATP, DNA or uric acid (which crystallizes once outside the cell), and signals danger when it spills out of damaged or dying cells.
Introduced in Chapter 2 · Innate Immunity
Dendritic cell
A star-shaped immune cell that takes up antigens in tissues and carries them to a lymph node, where it presents peptides from them on MHC molecules to T cells. Once it has sensed danger, it also expresses the costimulatory molecules needed to activate naive T cells.
Introduced in Chapter 1 · Cells, Proteins and Molecular Recognition
Dissociation constant (KD)
The usual measure of affinity: the concentration of ligand at which half the receptors are occupied at any moment. The smaller the KD, the tighter the binding. It equals the off-rate (how quickly bound partners separate) divided by the on-rate (how quickly they bind).
Introduced in Chapter 1 · Cells, Proteins and Molecular Recognition
DLL3
A protein displayed on the surface of many small-cell lung cancers. It is the target of tarlatamab.
Introduced in Chapter 9 · Therapeutic Antibodies
DNA
The long, double-stranded molecule that stores genetic information as a sequence of four bases, written A, C, G and T. Each strand is a chain of nucleotides, and the bases of the two strands pair up, A with T and C with G.
Introduced in Chapter 1 · Cells, Proteins and Molecular Recognition
Driver mutation
A mutation that gives a cell a growth or survival advantage, pushing it toward cancer. A typical tumor carries roughly two to eight.
Introduced in Chapter 6 · Cancer Cells and Tumor Antigens
Drug-to-antibody ratio (DAR)
The average number of payload molecules attached to each antibody in an ADC.
Introduced in Chapter 9 · Therapeutic Antibodies

E

Effector cell
An activated lymphocyte that does the immune work, such as a killer T cell or an antibody-secreting plasma cell.
Introduced in Chapter 3 · Adaptive Immunity
Effector T cell
An activated T cell ready to act, usually after leaving the lymph node; for a CD8 T cell, ready to kill.
Introduced in Chapter 5 · T Cells: Selection, Killing and Regulation
EGFR (epidermal growth factor receptor)
A growth-signal receptor related to HER2. It is the target of cetuximab and panitumumab.
Introduced in Chapter 9 · Therapeutic Antibodies
Endoplasmic reticulum (ER)
A folded internal membrane compartment where a cell assembles proteins destined for its surface. MHC class I molecules are loaded with their peptides there.
Introduced in Chapter 4 · Antigen Presentation
Endosome
A membrane-bound compartment inside a cell that receives material taken up from outside. Endosomes become more acidic as they mature and pass their contents to lysosomes for degradation; MHC class II molecules are loaded with peptides in these compartments.
Introduced in Chapter 4 · Antigen Presentation
Endothelium
The single layer of cells lining the inside of every blood vessel.
Introduced in Chapter 2 · Innate Immunity
Enzyme
A protein that speeds up a specific chemical reaction, such as cutting or joining other molecules.
Introduced in Chapter 1 · Cells, Proteins and Molecular Recognition
Epigenetic
Describes chemical and structural marks on DNA and its packaging that control which genes a cell can use, without changing the DNA sequence. They can make a cell’s identity stable.
Introduced in Chapter 5 · T Cells: Selection, Killing and Regulation
Epithelium
A sheet of tightly joined cells that covers the skin and lines the surfaces of organs and body cavities. Carcinomas, the most common cancers, arise from epithelial cells.
Introduced in Chapter 6 · Cancer Cells and Tumor Antigens
Epitope
The exact small patch on an antigen that a particular antibody or receptor binds. For a T cell, it is the peptide held in an MHC molecule.
Introduced in Chapter 3 · Adaptive Immunity
Epstein–Barr virus (EBV)
A very common herpesvirus — the cause of mononucleosis — that stays in the body for life. Kept in check by T cells, it can drive some lymphomas when immunity is weak.
Introduced in Chapter 7 · Immune Surveillance and Tumor Escape
Event-free survival
Whether, or for how long, patients stay alive without any defined setback — such as cancer growth, relapse, or being unable to have planned surgery.
Introduced in Chapter 12 · Resistance and New Directions
Extravasation
The process by which a white blood cell leaves the bloodstream: it rolls along the vessel wall as selectins engage their ligands, arrests when its integrins bind adhesion molecules on the vessel lining, and then migrates out between the lining cells.
Introduced in Chapter 2 · Innate Immunity

F

Fab arm
Either of the two identical arms of an antibody’s Y (Fab stands for “fragment, antigen-binding”). Each is made of one light chain and part of one heavy chain, and carries one antigen-binding site, formed by their variable regions, at its end.
Introduced in Chapter 3 · Adaptive Immunity
Fas and Fas ligand
A “death receptor” (Fas) found on many cells, and its ligand (Fas ligand), which killer T cells and some tumor blood vessels express; their binding triggers apoptosis in the Fas-bearing cell.
Introduced in Chapter 5 · T Cells: Selection, Killing and Regulation
Fc receptor
A receptor on immune cells such as macrophages, neutrophils and NK cells that binds the Fc region of antibodies bound to a target, so those cells can attack antibody-coated targets.
Introduced in Chapter 3 · Adaptive Immunity
Fc region
The stem of an antibody’s Y, formed by the lower parts of its two heavy chains’ constant regions (Fc stands for “fragment, crystallizable”). It does not bind the target; Fc receptors on immune cells and complement proteins bind it, and its binding to the recycling receptor FcRn determines how long the antibody lasts in the blood.
Introduced in Chapter 3 · Adaptive Immunity
Fecal microbiota transplant (FMT)
Transfer of stool from a screened donor into a patient’s gut, to replace their gut bacterial community.
Introduced in Chapter 12 · Resistance and New Directions
FOXP3
The transcription factor that gives regulatory T cells their identity. Mutations in its gene cause the scurfy disease in mice and IPEX in humans.
Introduced in Chapter 5 · T Cells: Selection, Killing and Regulation
Frameshift mutation
An insertion or deletion of DNA bases (not in a multiple of three) that shifts how the sequence is read in three-base codons, changing every amino acid after it.
Introduced in Chapter 6 · Cancer Cells and Tumor Antigens

G

γδ (gamma-delta) T cell
A minority family of T cells whose receptors are built from different chains (gamma and delta) than ordinary T cells’. They recognize signs of stress and do not need MHC class I, so they can attack cells that have lost it.
Introduced in Chapter 4 · Antigen Presentation
Gene
A stretch of DNA whose sequence encodes a protein (or, for some genes, a functional RNA molecule).
Introduced in Chapter 1 · Cells, Proteins and Molecular Recognition
Gene amplification
The presence of extra copies of a gene, sometimes dozens, so that a cell makes far more of its protein than normal.
Introduced in Chapter 6 · Cancer Cells and Tumor Antigens
Gene expression
The use of a gene to make its product: the gene is copied into RNA and, for a protein-coding gene, the RNA is read to build the protein. A cell is said to express the proteins it makes.
Introduced in Chapter 1 · Cells, Proteins and Molecular Recognition
Gene segment
One of many alternative pieces of DNA (V, D or J) that lymphocytes cut and join to build a complete receptor gene.
Introduced in Chapter 3 · Adaptive Immunity
Genome
The complete set of an organism’s DNA. In humans it is about 3 billion base pairs, present in two copies in nearly every cell that has a nucleus.
Introduced in Chapter 1 · Cells, Proteins and Molecular Recognition
Germinal center
A temporary structure that forms in lymph nodes and the spleen during an immune response, where B cells mutate their antibody genes and compete to be selected.
Introduced in Chapter 3 · Adaptive Immunity
GM-CSF
Granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor, a cytokine that recruits and matures dendritic cells; some vaccines and oncolytic viruses carry its gene.

Proteasome
A barrel-shaped enzyme complex inside every cell that degrades unwanted or faulty proteins by cleaving them into short peptides. Most of these are broken down further to amino acids and recycled; a few are carried into the endoplasmic reticulum by TAP and presented on MHC class I.
Introduced in Chapter 4 · Antigen Presentation
Protein
A chain of amino acids folded into a specific three-dimensional shape. Proteins do most of the work in cells, and their shape determines what they do and what they bind.
Introduced in Chapter 1 · Cells, Proteins and Molecular Recognition
Pseudoprogression
Apparent growth of tumors on scans early in immunotherapy, from an influx of immune cells or a delayed response, followed by shrinkage.
Introduced in Chapter 8 · Checkpoint Inhibitors

R

RAG genes
Genes encoding the pair of enzymes that cut DNA so that receptor genes can be assembled from segments (V(D)J recombination) in developing T and B cells; without them, an animal has no T or B cells.
Introduced in Chapter 7 · Immune Surveillance and Tumor Escape
RAG1 and RAG2
The pair of enzymes, active only in developing lymphocytes, that cut DNA so that V, D and J gene segments can be joined.
Introduced in Chapter 3 · Adaptive Immunity
Randomized trial
A study in which chance decides which treatment each patient receives, so the groups are alike and differences in outcome can be credited to the treatment.
Introduced in Chapter 8 · Checkpoint Inhibitors
Receptor
A protein, often spanning the cell membrane, that binds a specific molecule (its ligand) and relays a signal into the cell.
Introduced in Chapter 1 · Cells, Proteins and Molecular Recognition
Receptor occupancy
The share of a drug’s target molecules that have drug bound at a given moment. For PD-1 blockers it is often measured on blood T cells; it shows how completely, and for how long, the drug blocks its target.
Introduced in Chapter 8 · Checkpoint Inhibitors
Recurrence-free survival
The time from the start of treatment until the cancer comes back or the patient dies, whichever comes first.
Introduced in Chapter 11 · Cancer Vaccines and Oncolytic Viruses
Refractory
Describes a cancer that does not respond to treatment, or has stopped responding. “Relapsed” means it came back after a response.
Introduced in Chapter 10 · Cell Therapies
Regulatory T cell (Treg)
A type of CD4 T cell, defined by the protein FOXP3, that suppresses other immune cells to prevent autoimmunity. Tumors often recruit them.
Introduced in Chapter 5 · T Cells: Selection, Killing and Regulation
Repertoire
The full set of different receptors carried by one person’s B cells or T cells.
Introduced in Chapter 3 · Adaptive Immunity
Response rate (objective response rate)
The share of patients whose tumors shrink substantially (by at least 30% by standard measuring rules) or disappear.
Introduced in Interlude · 130 Years of Immunotherapy
Ribosome
The cell’s protein-building machine. It reads messenger RNA one codon (three bases) at a time and links the matching amino acids into a chain.
Introduced in Chapter 1 · Cells, Proteins and Molecular Recognition
RNA
A single-stranded chemical cousin of DNA that uses the base uracil (U) in place of thymine (T). Cells use it mainly as working copies of genes and in building proteins, for example as part of ribosomes.
Introduced in Chapter 1 · Cells, Proteins and Molecular Recognition

S

Sarcoma
A cancer that starts in connective tissue such as bone, muscle, fat or cartilage.
Introduced in Chapter 6 · Cancer Cells and Tumor Antigens
scFv (single-chain variable fragment)
The target-binding part of an antibody: the variable regions of its heavy and light chains joined by a short flexible linker into one protein chain. It determines what a CAR binds.
Introduced in Chapter 9 · Therapeutic Antibodies
Secondary response
A repeat response to an antigen seen before; faster and larger, with higher-affinity antibodies, because memory cells give it a head start.
Introduced in Chapter 3 · Adaptive Immunity
Selectin
An adhesion molecule whose bonds with its ligands form and break quickly, so that a white blood cell held by selectins rolls along a vessel wall instead of stopping. E- and P-selectin appear on the lining of inflamed vessels and slow passing white blood cells; L-selectin sits on white blood cells, notably naive lymphocytes, which use it to enter lymph nodes through high endothelial venules.
Introduced in Chapter 2 · Innate Immunity
Self
To the immune system, the molecules made by your own body that it has learned to tolerate. “Non-self” is everything else.
Introduced in Chapter 1 · Cells, Proteins and Molecular Recognition
Sepsis
A life-threatening condition in which the body’s response to an infection runs out of control and damages its own organs, causing widespread vessel leakage and low blood pressure. The infection itself need not be in the blood.
Introduced in Chapter 2 · Innate Immunity
Sequencing
Reading the order of nucleotides (the “letters”, or bases) in DNA or RNA. Comparing a tumor’s sequence with a normal sample reveals the tumor’s mutations.
Introduced in Chapter 11 · Cancer Vaccines and Oncolytic Viruses
Solid tumor
A cancer that grows as a mass in an organ or tissue, such as most cancers of the lung, breast, colon or skin; contrasted with blood cancers.
Introduced in Chapter 2 · Innate Immunity
Somatic hypermutation
Targeted mutation of the DNA encoding antibody variable regions in activated B cells, creating variants that are then selected for higher affinity.
Introduced in Chapter 3 · Adaptive Immunity
Spleen
An organ in the upper left abdomen that filters the blood and hosts large numbers of lymphocytes; it does for the blood what lymph nodes do for the tissues.
Introduced in Chapter 1 · Cells, Proteins and Molecular Recognition
Stem-like T cell (progenitor exhausted T cell)
A T cell found in chronic infections and tumors, marked by the transcription factor TCF1, that renews itself while giving rise to descendants that kill but become exhausted. It is the main source of new T cells after the PD-1 brake is released.
Introduced in Chapter 5 · T Cells: Selection, Killing and Regulation
Step-up dosing
Starting a drug at small doses and increasing in steps, which reduces the risk of cytokine release syndrome.
Introduced in Chapter 9 · Therapeutic Antibodies
STING
“Stimulator of interferon genes”: a signaling protein inside cells that is activated when its partner sensor, cGAS, detects DNA free in the cytoplasm (a sign of infection or damage), triggering type I interferon production.
Introduced in Chapter 12 · Resistance and New Directions
Stress ligand
A molecule, such as MICA or MICB, that cells display when infected, damaged or turning cancerous, and that activates NK cells.
Introduced in Chapter 2 · Innate Immunity
Stroma
The supportive tissue of an organ or tumor — fibroblasts, blood vessels and the fibrous scaffolding between cells.
Introduced in Chapter 7 · Immune Surveillance and Tumor Escape
SynNotch
A synthetic receptor that, on binding one antigen, induces expression of a gene — for example the gene for a CAR against a second antigen. It is how a two-marker “AND” rule is built into a cell.
Introduced in Chapter 10 · Cell Therapies

T

T cell
A lymphocyte that matures in the thymus (hence “T”) and uses its T-cell receptor to recognize peptides presented on MHC molecules. Killer (CD8) T cells destroy infected or cancerous cells; helper (CD4) T cells coordinate the response.
Introduced in Chapter 1 · Cells, Proteins and Molecular Recognition
T-cell engager
A two-armed molecule, usually a bispecific antibody, that binds CD3 on a T cell with one arm and a target on a cancer cell with the other, physically bridging the two cells so that the T cell kills the cancer cell.
Introduced in Interlude · 130 Years of Immunotherapy
T-cell exhaustion
A stable state of reduced function that T cells enter under long, unrelenting stimulation, as in chronic infections and tumors. Exhausted cells express many inhibitory receptors such as PD-1, divide little and kill poorly.
Introduced in Chapter 5 · T Cells: Selection, Killing and Regulation
T-cell receptor (TCR)
The recognition molecule on a T cell, assembled by the same gene rearrangement as antibodies (V(D)J recombination) and unique to each T cell and its clone. It recognizes a specific peptide held in a specific MHC (HLA) molecule and, unlike an antibody, is never secreted.
Introduced in Chapter 3 · Adaptive Immunity
TAP (transporter associated with antigen processing)
A pump in the membrane of the endoplasmic reticulum that carries peptides produced by the proteasome into the ER, where they can be loaded onto MHC class I molecules.
Introduced in Chapter 4 · Antigen Presentation
TCR-T cell
A T cell engineered to express a T-cell receptor cloned from a person whose immune system recognized a particular tumor peptide, often with its affinity increased in the laboratory. Unlike a CAR, it can recognize peptides from proteins made inside the cell, but only in patients with a matching HLA type.
Introduced in Interlude · 130 Years of Immunotherapy
Tertiary lymphoid structure (TLS)
An organized cluster of B cells, T cells and dendritic cells that forms inside inflamed tissue, including some tumors — a miniature lymph node.
Introduced in Chapter 7 · Immune Surveillance and Tumor Escape
TGF-β (transforming growth factor beta)
A cytokine that promotes scarring (fibrosis) and tissue repair and suppresses immune responses. Tumors use it to build fibrous barriers that keep T cells out, and to suppress T cells directly.
Introduced in Chapter 5 · T Cells: Selection, Killing and Regulation
Th1 (T helper 1) cell
A type of helper T cell that makes IFN-γ, activates macrophages and supports killer T cells; important against viruses and tumors.
Introduced in Chapter 5 · T Cells: Selection, Killing and Regulation
Therapeutic vaccine
A vaccine given to someone who already has a disease, to strengthen the immune attack on it, for example on an existing tumor.
Introduced in Chapter 11 · Cancer Vaccines and Oncolytic Viruses
Thymocyte
A developing T cell inside the thymus, before it has passed selection.
Introduced in Chapter 5 · T Cells: Selection, Killing and Regulation
Thymus
A small organ behind the breastbone, in front of the heart, where T cells develop and are tested so that most self-reactive ones are removed. It is most active early in life and gradually shrinks with age.
Introduced in Chapter 1 · Cells, Proteins and Molecular Recognition
TIGIT
An inhibitory receptor on T cells and NK cells. Antibodies against it looked promising in early trials but failed in large ones.
Introduced in Chapter 8 · Checkpoint Inhibitors
Tissue
A group of similar cells working together, such as muscle, skin or the lining of the gut.
Introduced in Chapter 1 · Cells, Proteins and Molecular Recognition
Tissue-agnostic approval
Approval of a drug for tumors with a particular molecular feature, such as MSI-high status, regardless of the organ where the cancer began.
Introduced in Interlude · 130 Years of Immunotherapy
Tolerance
The immune system’s learned restraint toward the body’s own molecules (and some harmless foreign ones, such as food). It is maintained by deleting or silencing self-reactive lymphocytes, both where they develop (central tolerance) and throughout the body (peripheral tolerance).
Introduced in Chapter 1 · Cells, Proteins and Molecular Recognition
Toll-like receptor (TLR)
A family of ten pattern-recognition receptors in humans, located on the cell surface or inside endosomes. Each recognizes a different microbial signature, such as LPS (TLR4) or viral RNA (TLR7 and TLR8).
Introduced in Chapter 2 · Innate Immunity
TOX
A transcription factor induced by strong, persistent T-cell receptor signaling that sets up the exhausted state and is needed for exhausted T cells, including the stem-like reserve, to persist.
Introduced in Chapter 5 · T Cells: Selection, Killing and Regulation
Trained immunity
A temporary, non-specific heightened state of innate immune cells after certain infections or vaccines, produced by epigenetic and metabolic reprogramming.
Introduced in Chapter 2 · Innate Immunity
Transcription factor
A protein that binds DNA and controls which genes a cell expresses. Some set a cell’s type and keep it stable, such as FOXP3 in regulatory T cells, T-bet in Th1 cells and TOX in exhausted T cells.
Introduced in Chapter 5 · T Cells: Selection, Killing and Regulation
Transduction
Delivery of a gene into a cell by a viral vector. In CAR-T manufacturing, the CAR gene is transduced into the patient’s T cells, where it integrates into their DNA.
Introduced in Chapter 10 · Cell Therapies
Tumor
An abnormal mass of cells growing beyond the body’s normal controls. Benign tumors stay put; malignant ones (cancers) can invade surrounding tissue and spread.
Introduced in Chapter 5 · T Cells: Selection, Killing and Regulation
Tumor antigen
Any molecule that lets the immune system tell a tumor cell from a healthy one, such as a neoantigen, a viral protein, a cancer-testis antigen or an overexpressed self protein.
Introduced in Chapter 6 · Cancer Cells and Tumor Antigens
Tumor heterogeneity
Differences between the cells of one tumor, which arise as different branches of its family tree pick up different mutations.
Introduced in Chapter 6 · Cancer Cells and Tumor Antigens
Tumor microenvironment
Everything in and around a tumor that is not a cancer cell: blood vessels, fibroblasts, immune cells, signaling molecules and the fibers between cells, often arranged in ways that suppress immunity.
Introduced in Chapter 6 · Cancer Cells and Tumor Antigens
Tumor mutational burden (TMB)
The number of mutations in a tumor’s DNA, usually counted per million DNA bases (a megabase) sequenced. A high count often, but not always, means more neoantigens, and numbers from different tests are not directly comparable.
Introduced in Chapter 6 · Cancer Cells and Tumor Antigens
Tumor necrosis factor (TNF)
An inflammatory cytokine released by T cells and macrophages that activates blood vessels and immune cells and can kill some tumor cells.
Introduced in Chapter 2 · Innate Immunity
Tumor suppressor
A gene whose normal job is to restrain cell division, repair DNA or trigger apoptosis: a growth brake inside the cell, such as TP53 or RB1. Not to be confused with immune checkpoints, which are brakes on T cells.
Introduced in Chapter 6 · Cancer Cells and Tumor Antigens
Tumor-associated antigen
A normal self protein that tumor cells make in excess, or that belongs to their tissue of origin, such as HER2 or pigment-cell proteins. Healthy cells carry it too.
Introduced in Chapter 6 · Cancer Cells and Tumor Antigens
Tumor-associated macrophage (TAM)
A macrophage living in a tumor, often pushed into a wound-healing (“M2-like”) state in which it helps the tumor build blood vessels and suppresses T cells.
Introduced in Chapter 2 · Innate Immunity
Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TIL)
T cells harvested from inside a patient’s own tumor, grown to billions in the laboratory and infused back as a treatment. Their receptors are the patient’s own and are not engineered.
Introduced in Interlude · 130 Years of Immunotherapy

V

V(D)J recombination
The process by which a developing lymphocyte cuts its DNA and joins one V, (one D) and one J gene segment, creating a new receptor gene.
Introduced in Chapter 3 · Adaptive Immunity
Vaccine
A preparation that exposes the immune system to a target in advance, such as a harmless version or piece of a pathogen, or the mRNA to make one, so that the B and T cells that recognize it expand and leave memory cells behind. Cancer vaccines can prevent virus-caused cancers or try to treat existing tumors.
Introduced in Chapter 3 · Adaptive Immunity
Variable region
The part of an antibody or T-cell receptor chain whose amino-acid sequence differs from one receptor to the next, encoded by rearranged V, (D) and J gene segments. At the end of each antibody arm, the variable regions of one heavy and one light chain together form the antigen-binding site.
Introduced in Chapter 3 · Adaptive Immunity
VEGF (vascular endothelial growth factor)
A signaling protein that makes blood vessels sprout new branches. Tumors overproduce it to build their blood supply, creating leaky, disorganized vessels that also hinder immune cells.
Introduced in Chapter 9 · Therapeutic Antibodies
Viral vector
A virus stripped of the genes it needs to replicate and cause disease, used to carry a chosen gene into cells. Lentiviral and retroviral vectors integrate the gene permanently into the cell’s chromosomes.
Introduced in Chapter 10 · Cell Therapies
Virus-like particle (VLP)
An empty viral shell (capsid) assembled from a virus’s coat protein. It looks like the virus to the immune system but contains no genetic material, so it cannot infect.
Introduced in Chapter 11 · Cancer Vaccines and Oncolytic Viruses
Vitiligo
Patchy loss of skin color, caused when the immune system destroys pigment-producing cells.
Introduced in Chapter 6 · Cancer Cells and Tumor Antigens

W

White blood cell (leukocyte)
Any immune cell. The name comes from their pale color compared with red blood cells. Most white blood cells live outside the blood, in the bone marrow, the other lymphoid organs and the tissues.
Introduced in Chapter 1 · Cells, Proteins and Molecular Recognition
