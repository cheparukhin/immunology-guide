# Aggregate scientific review — "Self & Other"

Chief scientific reviewer, whole-book pass. State of knowledge checked against October 2026.
Sources read: `docs/PLAN.md`, `docs/aggregate/book-reading-copy.md` (all 13 chapters in order), `docs/aggregate/all-figure-specs.md`, and the relevant passages of `content/drafts/*.md`. Running notes: `docs/aggregate/science-ledger.md`.

---

## Executive summary

Read end to end, this is an accurate, unusually honest book. The spine — recognition by molecular touch → a random receptor library → MHC display → killers held by brakes → cancer as altered self → immunoediting → the cancer-immunity cycle → therapies that repair or bypass particular links — is sound and holds together across 13 chapters. Mechanisms are explained compatibly in nearly every case: CTLA-4 vs PD-1, MHC-I/HLA and cross-presentation, exhaustion with its TCF1+ reserve, antigen classes, affinity vs selectivity, MHC-independence of CARs vs HLA restriction of TCR-T. Recurring numbers match where they should. Evidence levels are labeled with real discipline — mouse vs human, single-arm vs randomized, peer-reviewed vs company-reported — and the account of benefit, toxicity, cost and failure is more balanced than most professional reviews.

Three aggregate problems need fixing. (1) The book's canonical teaching device, the cancer-immunity cycle, assigns anti-PD-1 to the killing step alone; Chapter 8's own account (a reserve in lymph nodes and blood, newly arriving clones) and Chapter 12's neoadjuvant argument contradict it. (2) "No danger signal, no response" hardens into a claim that tumors are immunologically silent, which sits badly against the same book's wound-like, macrophage-filled tumors and its working immunoediting. (3) A duplicated paragraph in Chapter 10. Plus twelve smaller fixes and a few real gaps (tumor-intrinsic resistance signaling, NK-directed therapy outcomes, surrogate endpoints).

## Overall accuracy verdict

**Scientifically accurate and current as of October 2026; publishable after the MUST fixes.** No chapter contains a mechanism that is simply wrong. The defects are cross-chapter coherence, two over-absolute framings, and a handful of 2025–2026 updates.

## MUST fix

### M1. Anti-PD-1 is confined to "killing" (step 7), contradicting Chapter 8 and Chapter 12
**Affects: `content/drafts/07-escape.md` (main text + the ch07-cycle therapy data), `docs/aggregate/all-figure-specs.md` (ch07-cycle, reused as ch12-resistance and ch12-combinations), `content/drafts/12-frontier.md`; cross-listed under `content/drafts/08-checkpoints.md`.**

Locations and quotes:
- `07-escape.md` L312: "among the {{checkpoint|checkpoint}} inhibitors, anti-CTLA-4 acts at priming and anti-PD-1 at killing (Chapter 8)".
- `all-figure-specs.md` L2268 (ch07-cycle THERAPIES): `anti-pd1 | Anti-PD-1 / PD-L1 | true | 7 | — | — | — | Release the PD-1 brake on T cells already in the tumor.` (`acts = 7` only).
- `07-escape.md` L549: "Checkpoint inhibitors release brakes on T cells that are already there".
- `12-frontier.md` L139 (key idea): "releasing the PD-1 brake mainly repairs the last one"; L19: "act mainly on the last link".

Problem: Chapter 8 states the opposite in its own voice — "The reserve also lives in lymph nodes and blood… many clones that expanded in the tumor had not been detectable there before, as if it were being resupplied from elsewhere" (`08-checkpoints.md`, "Two brakes, two places"), and its deep dive concludes "PD-1 blockade works less by reviving the exhausted than by unleashing a reserve, in the tumor, the lymph nodes and the blood". Chapter 12's neoadjuvant section then argues that treating while the tumor (and its draining nodes) are still in place primes *more* T-cell clones — an effect located at steps 2–3, not step 7. So a reader who learns the wheel in Chapter 7, then meets Chapters 8 and 12, is told two incompatible things, and the figure that is reused in Chapter 12 encodes the narrower claim. The literature is clear that PD-1/PD-L1 blockade also acts on priming in tumor-draining lymph nodes.

Evidence: Dammeijer et al., *Cancer Cell* 2020, "The PD-1/PD-L1-Checkpoint Restrains T cell Immunity in Tumor-Draining Lymph Nodes" (PMID 33007259, doi:10.1016/j.ccell.2020.09.001; AMBC_1XKHT69fWiq7bj708tkogAtKYTv). Huang et al., *Cell* 2022, "The primordial differentiation of tumor-specific memory CD8+ T cells as bona fide responders to PD-1/PD-L1 blockade in draining lymph nodes" (PMID 36208623, doi:10.1016/j.cell.2022.09.020). Yost et al., *Nat Med* 2019, clonal replacement (PMID 31359002, doi:10.1038/s41591-019-0522-3) — already cited in Chapter 8.

Rewrite (concrete):
1. `07-escape.md` L312 → "among the {{checkpoint|checkpoint}} inhibitors, anti-CTLA-4 acts mainly at priming and anti-PD-1 mainly at killing — though, as Chapter 8 explains, PD-1 blockade also frees T cells being primed in the lymph nodes that drain the tumor, so it acts at step 3 as well; vaccines boost presentation and priming (Chapter 11)."
2. ch07-cycle THERAPIES row → `anti-pd1 | Anti-PD-1 / PD-L1 | true | 7, 3 | — | — | — | Releases the PD-1 brake on T cells in the tumor, and also on T cells being primed in the tumor's draining lymph nodes. | Approved | Ch 8`, with step 7 drawn as the primary site (full ring) and step 3 as secondary (thin ring), matching the "mainly/also" wording.
3. `07-escape.md` L549 → "Checkpoint inhibitors release brakes on T cells that already recognize the tumor — mostly those inside it, plus others still being primed in the draining lymph nodes — so responses occur most often in inflamed tumors…".
4. `12-frontier.md` L139 → "…and releasing the PD-1 brake mainly repairs the last link, with some help at priming."

### M2. "No danger signal, no response" hardens into "tumors are immunologically silent", which the book elsewhere contradicts
**Affects: `content/drafts/02-innate.md` (key idea L534; L547), `content/drafts/06-cancer.md` (L222), `content/drafts/07-escape.md` ("A patrol for rogue cells"; the wound/fortified-neighborhood framing), `content/drafts/11-vaccines.md` (L118, weak link 2).**

Quotes:
- `02-innate.md` L534: "No danger signal, no response. The innate system decides whether the adaptive system acts at all, and a tumor, built from the body's own molecules, rarely raises the alarm."
- `02-innate.md` L547: "**It largely misses cancer**, which carries few danger signals of any kind."
- `06-cancer.md` L222: "Worse, a young tumor rarely sounds an alarm. It carries no bacterial or viral patterns, and early on it causes little damage."

Problem: this is the book's clearest accumulated misconception. Two chapters earlier and later, the same book says tumors are "wounds that do not heal", are packed with tumor-associated macrophages and MDSCs, and that chronic inflammation *causes* cancer (`02-innate.md`, "Fighters and builders"); Chapter 7 then asserts that the immune system eliminates many nascent tumors and holds others in equilibrium for years — which requires that priming does happen, repeatedly, without any microbe. A careful reader cannot reconcile "few danger signals of any kind" with "immunoediting", "inflamed tumors full of T cells", or Chapter 2's own cGAS–STING paragraph. The missing distinction is between *immunostimulatory* innate signaling (tumor DNA → cGAS–STING → type I interferon → cDC1 maturation and CD8 priming) and *tumor-promoting, suppressive* inflammation (IL-1β/IL-6, TGF-β, wound-healing macrophages), which is abundant in tumors. As written, Chapter 11's "alarms too weak" framework inherits the same flaw.

Evidence: Hanahan & Weinberg, *Cell* 2011 — "tumor-promoting inflammation" is an enabling characteristic, i.e. tumors are typically inflamed (already cited in Ch 6). Woo et al., *Immunity* 2014, STING-dependent cytosolic DNA sensing of immunogenic tumors (PMID 25517615, doi:10.1016/j.immuni.2014.10.017). Fuertes et al., *J Exp Med* 2011, host type I IFN via CD8α+ DCs is required for spontaneous anti-tumor CD8 responses (PMID 21930765, doi:10.1084/jem.20101159) and Diamond et al., same issue (PMID 21930769). Coussens & Werb, *Nature* 2002, inflammation and cancer.

Rewrite (concrete): in `02-innate.md`, change the key idea to separate the two kinds of signal, e.g. "No danger signal, no response. The innate system decides whether the adaptive system acts at all. Tumors are not quiet places — they are often inflamed, in the wound-healing way described above — but they are poor at producing the *particular* alarms that mature dendritic cells and license T-cell attack. Chapter 7 shows that this sometimes happens anyway." Then soften L547 to "**It often misses cancer**, which produces few of the alarms that license a T-cell attack, even while it provokes plenty of the wound-healing kind of inflammation. Tumor DNA reaching a dendritic cell's internal sensor (cGAS–STING) is one of the few routes that does work, and in mice it is required for spontaneous anti-tumor T cells." Add one sentence of the same distinction to `06-cancer.md` L222 ("no alarm of the kind that licenses killers — though as Chapter 7 shows, enough alarm gets through often enough that the immune system shapes most tumors before anyone sees them") and one clause to `11-vaccines.md` L118 ("the alarms that matter here are the ones that mature dendritic cells, not inflammation in general — tumors have plenty of the latter").

## SHOULD fix

### S1. Chapter 4's back-reference to Chapter 2 is wrong
`04-presentation.md` L37: "Chapter 2 called it an ID badge, because NK cells check that it is there." Chapter 2 introduces MHC class I as the **shop window** (`02-innate.md` L361: "Think of them as the cell's **shop window**"); "ID badges" appears only in Chapter 1's cell-as-city passage about membrane proteins in general. Fix: "Chapter 2 introduced it as the cell's shop window, and showed that NK cells check whether the window is there at all. Up close, the window turns out to be…". (Keeps the canonical metaphor table in `PLAN.md` §2 intact.)

### S2. Class I peptide length is given two ways
`04-presentation.md` (owner) says 8–10 amino acids throughout (L43, L126, L281, L547…); `03-adaptive.md` L502 and `11-vaccines.md` L273/L289 say 8–11 ("class I fragments, which are 8 to 11 amino acids long"). Both are defensible, but the book should say one thing. Fix: adopt Chapter 4's "usually 8–10 (occasionally up to about 11)" and make Chapters 3 and 11 match, or add the parenthesis in Chapter 4 and use "8–11" consistently elsewhere.

### S3. Precursor-frequency range differs between the owning chapter and Chapter 9
`04-presentation.md` L411: "the range runs from roughly **1 in 10,000 to 1 in a million**"; `09-antibodies.md` L313: "for any given target only about one T cell in 100,000 to a million matches". Fix: in Chapter 9 write "only about one T cell in 10,000 to a million — usually toward the rare end (Chapter 4)". The figure spec ch09-bridge L364 ("about one in 100,000 to a million") should match.

### S4. "Every checkpoint inhibitor approved so far is a monoclonal antibody… whose tips fit one target and nothing else" is no longer globally true
`08-checkpoints.md` L33. Chapter 12 itself describes ivonescimab, an approved-in-China PD-1 × VEGF **bispecific**, and cadonilimab (PD-1 × CTLA-4) has been approved in China since 2022 (Lee & Keam, *Drugs* 2022, PMID 35986837, doi:10.1007/s40265-022-01761-9). Fix: "Every checkpoint inhibitor approved in the US and Europe is a monoclonal antibody… (In China, two-in-one antibodies that block a checkpoint and a second target are already approved; Chapter 12.)"

### S5. Chapter 8 overstates the absence of an "attack under way" test
`08-checkpoints.md` L364: "One of the best clues is whether an attack is already under way — a 'hot' tumor — and no test captures that reliably yet." Two measures do capture it imperfectly but reproducibly, and `PLAN.md` §3 asks for one of them by name ("T-cell inflamed signatures"): the 18-gene T-cell–inflamed gene-expression profile (Ayers et al., *J Clin Invest* 2017, PMID 28650338, doi:10.1172/JCI91190) and the Immunoscore that Chapter 7 already describes. Fix: "…and no test captures it reliably enough for routine use. Research assays come closer: an 18-gene 'T-cell–inflamed' signature predicts response across several cancers, and the Immunoscore of Chapter 7 counts T cells directly — but neither is standard practice."

### S6. Currency: subcutaneous checkpoint inhibitors are not mentioned
`08-checkpoints.md` clinic box: "For most patients, checkpoint therapy is an infusion in an outpatient clinic every few weeks". Since December 2024 (nivolumab with hyaluronidase, Opdivo Qvantig; FDA first authorization 2024-12-27, Amass AMRC_VvmKuW7VweLjJOSngst9DwsPUCE) and September 2025 (pembrolizumab with berahyaluronidase alfa, Keytruda Qlex; FDA 2025-09-19, Amass AMRC_W95qodj0u9MrujqFqPVoyf4psoi), both main PD-1 blockers also exist as a few-minute subcutaneous injection. Fix: add "— or, increasingly, a subcutaneous injection that takes a few minutes, now approved for both main PD-1 blockers."

### S7. Currency: tarlatamab has moved into first-line maintenance (company-reported)
`09-antibodies.md` presents tarlatamab only "once first-line chemotherapy fails". On 8 September 2026 Amgen reported that phase 3 DeLLphi-305 (563 patients, tarlatamab + durvalumab vs durvalumab as first-line maintenance in extensive-stage SCLC) met its primary overall-survival endpoint at interim analysis; numbers were not released (company-reported, pending a congress). Fix: one sentence after the DeLLphi-304 numbers — "In September 2026 the makers reported that adding tarlatamab to durvalumab after first-line chemotherapy also lengthened survival in a phase 3 trial; no numbers had been released." Label as company-reported, as the book does elsewhere. (Full approval in November 2025 is already stated correctly: FDA traditional approval 19 Nov 2025, DeLLphi-304 median OS 13.6 vs 8.3 months, HR 0.60.)

### S8. Currency: the ivonescimab regulatory picture
`12-frontier.md` "PD-1 × VEGF" box says only that global trials are running. A US decision is pending: the FDA accepted the BLA (ivonescimab + chemotherapy, EGFR-mutant NSCLC after TKI, from the global HARMONi trial) on 29 January 2026 with a PDUFA goal date of 14 November 2026. Fix: add "A US decision on the EGFR-mutant indication is due in November 2026." Keeps the honest framing ("outside China the picture is less clear") while telling the reader where it stands.

### S9. Chapter 2's forward reference to NKG2A blockade is never paid off — and the lead drug failed
`02-innate.md` L461 lists "antibodies that block NKG2A" among approaches under investigation "(Chapter 12)". Chapter 12 mentions only "antibodies that recruit NK cells". Meanwhile the lead NKG2A antibody, monalizumab, failed its phase 3 in head and neck cancer: INTERLINK-1 was stopped for futility (median OS 8.8 vs 8.6 months; Clin Cancer Res 2025, PMID 40300079, doi:10.1158/1078-0432.CCR-25-0073); a phase 3 in stage III lung cancer (PACIFIC-9) continues. Fix: either add one clause to Chapter 12's scoreboard ("blocking NKG2A, an NK-cell brake, failed in a large head-and-neck trial; another phase 3 in lung cancer continues") or soften Chapter 2 to "is being tested" without the forward link. The first is better — it also fills gap G2.

### S10. Infection-attributable cancer fraction is phrased two ways
`06-cancer.md` L31: "about 2.2 million cancers a year worldwide, roughly one in eight"; `11-vaccines.md` L3070: "more than one in ten new cancers worldwide, about 2.2 million cases in 2018". Both round the same 13% figure, but a reader comparing them will stumble. Fix: use "about one in eight (13%)" in both, with the 2018 date in both.

### S11. Precision: the HLA allele count
`04-presentation.md` L920: "the reference catalog listed more than 45,000 HLA alleles by mid-2026". IPD-IMGT/HLA release 3.63 (January 2026) listed 44,876 HLA alleles; mid-2026 releases are just above 45,000, so the claim is plausible but on the edge. Fix: cite the release explicitly ("more than 45,000 by the mid-2026 release of the IPD-IMGT/HLA database") so the number is auditable, or retreat to "more than 44,000".

### S12. Chapter 8's "newcomers" inference deserves one hedge
`08-checkpoints.md`: "These newcomers are not a new kind of T cell: their receptors could already recognize the tumor; they had simply not yet joined the fight." Yost et al. showed that the expanded clones had not been *detected* in the tumor beforehand and carried tumor-reactive phenotypes; whether each had already been primed is inferred. Fix: "…their receptors fit the tumor; they had simply not yet been detected in it — whether each had already been primed elsewhere is inferred rather than shown."

## Gaps worth filling

**G1. Tumor-intrinsic oncogenic signaling as a resistance mechanism (owner: Ch 12, "How tumors fight back", two sentences; optionally one clause in Ch 7's "Go deaf").** The book covers B2M, HLA LOH, JAK1/2 and β-catenin-driven dendritic-cell exclusion, but never says that ordinary cancer-driver genes themselves set the immune tone. Suggested addition: "Some resistance is written into the tumor's driver genes. Lung cancers with mutated *STK11/LKB1* respond poorly to PD-1 blockade even when they carry plenty of mutations, and loss of the tumor suppressor PTEN makes mouse and human melanomas resist T-cell attack. The same mutations that drive growth can also set the immune temperature." Evidence: Skoulidis et al., *Cancer Discov* 2018 (PMID 29773717, doi:10.1158/2159-8290.CD-18-0099); Peng et al., *Cancer Discov* 2016, PTEN loss (PMID 26645196, doi:10.1158/2159-8290.CD-15-0283). This also strengthens Chapter 6's "two kinds of brake" key idea by showing the two systems are coupled.

**G2. NK-cell–directed therapy: what has been tried and what happened (owner: Ch 12 scoreboard, two sentences).** Chapter 2 builds missing-self carefully and promises Chapter 12; Chapter 10 covers CAR-NK; nothing reports outcomes. Suggested addition: NKG2A blockade (monalizumab) failed in phase 3 in head and neck cancer and continues in stage III lung cancer; NK-cell engagers (NKp46/CD16-based) are in early trials; CAR-NK remains experimental. See S9 for the citation.

**G3. Surrogate endpoints (owner: Ch 12, one short box or three sentences; alternatively Ch 11's "Reading the melanoma results").** The book's strongest recent results — NADINA and S1801 (event-free survival), KEYNOTE-942 and INTerpath-001 (recurrence-free survival), pathologic complete response in CheckMate 816, ctDNA clearance — are surrogates, and the book already warns about one of them ("a strong link between pathologic response and survival *within* a trial does not guarantee…"). It should say the general thing once: adjuvant and neoadjuvant immunotherapy is routinely approved on recurrence- or event-free survival, and a longer time to recurrence does not automatically mean longer life; adjuvant pembrolizumab in resected stage III melanoma, for example, was approved on recurrence-free survival (Eggermont et al., *J Clin Oncol* 2020, PMID 32946353, doi:10.1200/JCO.20.02110). One paragraph would immunize the reader against the 2026 vaccine headlines the book itself reports.

**G4. Myeloid cells as the tumor's dominant immune population (owner: Ch 7, one sentence in "Recruit corrupt guards").** Macrophages and other myeloid cells are frequently the most abundant immune cells in solid tumors, and they both suppress T cells and are required for some responses (antigen presentation, ADCC/phagocytosis of antibody-coated cells, IL-6 in CRS). The book treats them only as guards who have been corrupted. One sentence saying they are the largest immune population in many tumors, and double-edged, would make Chapter 9's ADCC, Chapter 10's IL-6 story and Chapter 12's CD47 failure cohere.

**G5. Endogenous antibodies and B cells in tumor immunity (owner: Ch 7, one clause in the main text).** The tertiary-lymphoid-structure deep dive is excellent but is the only place B cells appear on the tumor's side, so the main narrative implies tumor immunity is purely T-cell business. One clause — "tumors rich in B cells and these structures tend to respond better" — in the main text, pointing to the box, is enough.

**G6. Tissue-resident memory T cells (owner: Ch 5 or Ch 7, one sentence, optional).** A scientifically literate reader will miss the concept that some T cells take up permanent residence in tissues and are, in several cancers, the infiltrating population that correlates with survival. One sentence in Chapter 5's "The kill" or in Chapter 7's Immunoscore passage.

**G7. What "response" leaves out (owner: Ch 8, one clause, optional).** The book defines response as substantial shrinkage and notes "some others benefit without much shrinkage". Given how often it uses response rate as the headline number, one clause naming the alternative (durable disease stabilization, and why RECIST was built for chemotherapy) would help; the pseudoprogression box is the natural place.

## Verified — major cross-chapter claims I checked and confirmed

- **The CTLA-4 / PD-1 division of labor** is explained compatibly in Chapters 4, 5, 7, 8 and 12 (appearance after activation; competition with CD28 for B7 and trans-endocytosis; PD-L1 induced by IFN-γ; knockout phenotypes; and the repeated "mainly" hedge). Chapter 5's deep dive explicitly says the division of labor "describes where each brake matters most, not the only place it works" — which is why M1 is a fixable inconsistency rather than a conceptual error.
- **Exhaustion and what PD-1 blockade does.** Chapters 5, 8 and 12, plus the ch05-exhaustion and ch08-two-brakes specs, agree: a TOX-driven stable state; a TCF1+ progenitor reserve that is itself TOX+ and PD-1-positive; terminally exhausted cells barely responding; re-exhaustion if antigen persists; clonal replacement/revival in human tumors. This is current (2016–2022 literature) and better than most popular accounts.
- **MHC-I / HLA / cross-presentation.** Consistent across Chapters 2, 4, 5, 6, 7, 9, 10, 12: proteasome → TAP → ER loading → 8–10-mer display; HLA restriction; cDC1-dependent cross-presentation as the route to priming against tumors; B2M loss and HLA LOH as escape; CAR-T indifference to MHC loss vs TCR-T/tebentafusp dependence on it. The NK "missing self" counter-move is carried correctly from Chapter 2 into Chapters 7 and 12, and Chapter 12's γδ T-cell qualification is accurate: 20 of 21 B2M-inactivated mismatch-repair-deficient cancers still responded (de Vries et al., *Nature* 2023, PMID 36631610, doi:10.1038/s41586-022-05593-1).
- **Antigen classes and the three-way trade-off** (specific / shared / common) are consistent between Chapter 6, the ch06-antigen-kinds figure, Chapter 10 (MAGE-A4, titin cross-reactivity) and Chapter 11 (neoantigens vs MAGE-A3 in MAGRIT).
- **Affinity → selectivity trade-off** set up in Chapter 1 (TCR K_D 1–100 µM; engineered picomolar receptors less selective) pays off correctly in Chapter 10's MAGE-A3/titin deaths and in the obe-cel "intermediate-affinity binder" story.
- **The cancer-immunity cycle as the organizing device**: step assignments for vaccines (2, 3), anti-CTLA-4 (3), TGF-β blockade (5), CAR-T and engagers (skip 1–3, replace 6, enter at 4, still must travel/enter/kill) are correct and match Chapters 9, 10 and 11, including the deliberate rule that nothing skips steps 4, 5 or 7. Only the anti-PD-1 row is wrong (M1).
- **Recurring numbers match where they should**: 1.8 trillion immune cells / 1.2 kg / ~2% in blood (Ch 1, 5, 8); CheckMate 067 ten-year figures (19/37/43%; medians 19.9/36.9/71.9 months; HR 0.85, CI 0.69–1.05) used identically in Chapter 8 text, takeaways and the ch08-tail spec; 57 eligible / ~20 responding per 100 US patients with advanced cancer in Chapters 8, 12 and the interlude, correctly attributed to one group's estimate (Haslam, Olivier & Prasad, *Int J Cancer* 2025;156:2352–2359, doi:10.1002/ijc.35347 — the paper's own figures are 56.55% and 20.13% for 2023); dMMR in ~15% of colorectal cancers and ~1 in 20 metastatic (Ch 6, Ch 8).
- **2025–2026 developments are accurately stated and correctly labeled.** Spot-checked: INTerpath-001 topline, 1,137 patients randomized 2:1, RFS and distant-metastasis-free survival both met, announced 19 August 2026, no published numbers (NCT05933577; company-reported — the book says so); BioNTech's termination of BNT122-01 in ctDNA-positive resected colorectal cancer, 28 August 2026, futility boundary crossed October 2025, overall-survival imbalance noted by the monitoring board, pancreatic programme continuing; satri-cel conditional approval by China's NMPA on 22 June 2026, the world's first CAR-T for a solid tumor, with the book's deliberately unflattering PFS numbers (3.25 vs 1.77 months) correct; RP1 (vusolimogene oderparepvec) accelerated approval with nivolumab on 6 August 2026, ORR 24%; atezolizumab approved 15 May 2026 for ctDNA/MRD-positive muscle-invasive bladder cancer with a companion assay (IMvigor011); teclistamab + subcutaneous daratumumab approved 5 March 2026; tarlatamab traditional approval 19 November 2025; fianlimab's phase 3 miss (1,546 patients, 11.5 vs 6.4 months, HR 0.845, p = 0.063, company-reported, May 2026); SKYSCRAPER-01's negative result in exactly 521 patients (Peters et al., *J Clin Oncol* 2026;44:2400–2408, PMID 42507968 — the book's "521 people" and "did not significantly improve survival" are both right); HARMONi-2 overall survival 30.8 vs 22.6 months presented at WCLC, September 2026; lifileucel still on accelerated approval with TILVANCE-301 running and no European licence.
- **Balance and honesty.** Response rates, the tail of the curve, who does not benefit, fatal toxicity rates (1 in 270 / 1 in 90 / 1 in 80), permanent endocrine damage, CAR-T non-relapse mortality dominated by infection, cost ($12,000 a dose; $462,000–594,000 for CAR-T; 1–3% access in low- and middle-income countries), and the failure list (epacadostat, TIGIT, bempegaldesleukin, magrolimab, MAGRIT, T-VEC + pembrolizumab, bintrafusp, ELI-002, autogene cevumeran in colorectal cancer) are all present and proportionate. I found no systematic hype and no excessive pessimism; mouse-vs-human and single-arm-vs-randomized distinctions are made consistently, including inside figure captions.
