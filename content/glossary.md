# Canonical glossary (entries below; leave this heading)


- accelerated-approval | Accelerated approval | A US FDA pathway that approves a drug on early evidence, such as tumor shrinkage, on condition that later trials confirm a clinical benefit to patients. If they do not, the approval can be withdrawn.
- adaptive-immunity | Adaptive immunity | The slower, highly specific branch of the immune system, made up of T cells, B cells and the antibodies B cells secrete. It generates millions of different receptors in advance, expands the cells whose receptors bind a particular antigen (clonal expansion), and keeps memory cells that recognize that antigen for years.
- adc | Antibody–drug conjugate (ADC) | An antibody joined by a chemical linker to a potent cytotoxic (cell-killing) drug, the payload. The antibody delivers the payload to cells that express its target; these usually internalize the conjugate and release the drug inside.
- adcc | ADCC (antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity) | Killing of an antibody-coated cell by an immune cell, usually a natural killer (NK) cell, that binds the antibodies' Fc regions with its Fc receptors.
- adhesion-molecule | Adhesion molecule | A surface protein that binds a ligand on another cell or in the surrounding tissue, holding cells together or in place. Selectins, integrins and the ligands they bind are the adhesion molecules that let white blood cells leave the blood.
- adjuvant | Adjuvant | An ingredient added to a vaccine that activates innate danger sensors, such as pattern-recognition receptors, so that antigen-presenting cells mature and the adaptive response to the vaccine's antigen is strong. Not to be confused with adjuvant therapy, which is treatment after surgery.
- adjuvant-therapy | Adjuvant therapy | Treatment given after surgery to destroy any undetected cancer cells left behind and lower the risk of relapse. Not to be confused with a vaccine adjuvant.
- allele | Allele | One of the different versions of a gene that exist across a population. More than 45,000 HLA alleles were known by mid-2026.
- anchor-residue | Anchor residue | One of the few amino acids in a peptide (for MHC class I, often the second and the last) that must fit pockets in the floor of the peptide-binding groove for the peptide to stay bound. Different HLA versions have differently shaped pockets.
- anergy | Anergy | A lasting unresponsive state that a T cell enters when it recognizes its target without receiving the confirming (costimulatory) signal. The cell stays alive but no longer responds; this protects against autoimmunity but can also silence T cells able to attack a tumor.
- antibody | Antibody | A Y-shaped protein secreted by B cells; it is the secreted form of the B-cell receptor. Its two identical arms (Fab arms) bind one specific molecular feature, the epitope, and its stem (the Fc region) is bound by receptors on immune cells and by complement proteins. An antibody can neutralize its target or mark it for destruction.
- antigen | Antigen | Anything that the immune system's specific receptors (on T cells, on B cells, or antibodies) can recognize, such as part of a virus, a toxin or a protein from a cancer cell. It is often a protein or a fragment of one.
- antigen-escape | Antigen escape | Relapse of a cancer whose cells no longer carry a therapy's target in a form the therapy recognizes, because treatment selected for cells that lacked the target, lost it or changed it.
- antigen-presentation | Antigen presentation | The process by which a cell degrades proteins into short peptides and displays them on its surface in MHC molecules, so that T cells can detect what the cell is making or has taken up.
- antigen-presenting-cell | Professional antigen-presenting cell | A cell equipped with MHC class II and costimulatory molecules. Dendritic cells, macrophages and B cells are the main ones, but in practice only dendritic cells reliably activate a naive T cell; macrophages and B cells mostly interact with T cells that are already activated.
- apoptosis | Apoptosis | Programmed cell death, in which a cell dismantles itself into membrane-wrapped fragments that phagocytes engulf, without spilling its contents or causing inflammation.
- autoimmunity | Autoimmunity | An immune attack on the body's own healthy tissues, caused by a failure of tolerance, as in type 1 diabetes, multiple sclerosis or rheumatoid arthritis.
- avidity | Avidity | The overall binding strength of a molecule that binds its target at several sites at once, such as a two-armed antibody. It can be far greater than the affinity of any single site.
- b-cell | B cell | A lymphocyte that carries one kind of antibody on its surface as its receptor and, when activated, can differentiate into a plasma cell that secretes that antibody. B cells mature in the bone marrow; the "B" comes from the bursa of Fabricius, the bird organ where they were discovered.
- b2m | Beta-2-microglobulin (B2M) | A small protein subunit of every MHC class I molecule; without it, class I molecules cannot fold properly or reach the cell surface. Cells that lose B2M cannot present peptides to killer T cells.
- b7 | B7 (CD80 and CD86) | Two related proteins on dendritic cells and other antigen-presenting cells, whose expression rises sharply when danger signals make those cells mature. They deliver costimulation (signal 2) by binding the activating receptor CD28 on T cells, but the inhibitory receptor CTLA-4 binds them with higher affinity and competes with CD28 for them.
- bcg | BCG (bacillus Calmette–Guérin) | A weakened relative of the tuberculosis bacterium, used as a TB vaccine and, instilled into the bladder, as a treatment for early bladder cancer.
- bcma | BCMA (B-cell maturation antigen) | A surface protein on antibody-producing plasma cells and on the cancerous plasma cells of multiple myeloma. It is the target of several CAR-T therapies and T-cell engagers for myeloma.
- biomarker | Biomarker | A measurable feature, such as a protein, a gene change or a blood level, that indicates something about a disease or predicts how it will behave or respond to treatment.
- bispecific-antibody | Bispecific antibody | An engineered antibody with two different antigen-binding sites, so it can bind two different targets at once, for example a protein on a tumor cell and CD3 on a T cell.
- bone-marrow | Bone marrow | The soft tissue inside bones where blood cells are made, including the ancestors of all immune cells. B cells mature there, and it holds about 40% of the body's immune cells.
- cancer-immunity-cycle | Cancer-immunity cycle | A framework (Chen and Mellman, 2013) describing the seven steps of an immune attack on a tumor: antigen release, presentation by dendritic cells, priming in lymph nodes, trafficking, infiltration, recognition and killing. Killing releases more antigen, which can start the cycle again.
- car-t | CAR-T cell | A T cell, usually the patient's own, genetically engineered to express a chimeric antigen receptor (CAR), a synthetic receptor that joins an antibody's antigen-binding variable regions to T-cell signaling domains. Because the cells expand in the patient and can persist for years, they are called a "living drug".
- cd19 | CD19 | A protein on the surface of nearly every B cell, healthy or cancerous, including most B-cell leukemias and lymphomas. It is the target of blinatumomab and of the first successful CAR-T cells, chosen partly because people can live without B cells if given antibodies from donors.
- cd28 | CD28 | The main costimulatory (activating) receptor on T cells, the counterpart of inhibitory receptors such as CTLA-4. When it binds B7 on a mature dendritic cell while the T-cell receptor recognizes its target, it delivers signal 2, which lets a naive T cell activate instead of becoming anergic.
- cd3 | CD3 | The cluster of signaling chains bound to every T-cell receptor, together forming the TCR–CD3 complex. The receptor's antigen-binding chains cannot signal on their own; CD3 relays the activating signal into the cell. T-cell engagers (Chapter 9) bind CD3 to activate T cells.
- cd4-t-cell | Helper T cell (CD4 T cell) | A T cell whose CD4 co-receptor binds MHC class II, so it recognizes peptides presented by antigen-presenting cells. It coordinates immune responses, using cytokines and contact signals to help killer T cells, B cells and macrophages.
- cd8-t-cell | Killer T cell (CD8 T cell) | A T cell whose CD8 co-receptor binds MHC class I, so it can inspect the peptides presented by nearly any cell in the body and, once activated, kill cells that present its target, such as infected or cancerous cells. Also called a cytotoxic T cell.
- cell | Cell | The basic unit of life: a microscopic compartment wrapped in a membrane that uses energy, responds to its surroundings and, usually, can divide. An adult human has roughly 30 trillion.
- checkpoint | Immune checkpoint | An inhibitory receptor (a "brake") on immune cells, such as CTLA-4 or PD-1, that limits the strength and length of a response and protects healthy tissue from attack. Checkpoint inhibitor drugs block these receptors or their ligands, such as PD-L1.
- checkpoint-inhibitor | Checkpoint inhibitor | A drug, usually a monoclonal antibody, that blocks an immune checkpoint such as PD-1, its ligand PD-L1, or CTLA-4, releasing a brake on T cells so they can keep attacking.
- chemokine | Chemokine | A type of cytokine that attracts cells. Immune cells migrate along its concentration gradient toward the cells secreting it.
- chemotherapy | Chemotherapy | Drugs that kill rapidly dividing cells, usually by damaging DNA or blocking the process of cell division. They hit cancer cells but also harm healthy dividing cells, such as those in the bone marrow, gut lining and hair follicles.
- chimeric-antibody | Chimeric antibody | An engineered antibody in which a mouse antibody's variable regions, which bind the target, are joined to human constant regions. Classic names contain -xi-, as in rituximab. The name refers to the Chimera, a creature of Greek myth made from parts of different animals.
- chimeric-antigen-receptor | Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) | A laboratory-made receptor that combines an antibody's antigen-binding variable regions (an scFv), outside the cell, with T-cell signaling domains (CD3ζ plus, in approved products, one costimulatory domain), inside it, joined by a hinge and a transmembrane domain. "Chimeric" because it is assembled from parts of different molecules, after the Chimera of Greek myth, a creature made from parts of different animals.
- clinical-trial | Clinical trial | A study that tests a treatment in people, usually in phases: phase 1 tests safety and dose, phase 2 looks for early signs of benefit, and phase 3 compares the new treatment with the standard one in large, usually randomized groups.
- clonal-expansion | Clonal expansion | The rapid, repeated division of the rare lymphocytes whose receptors recognize a target, producing thousands to millions of identical cells (a clone).
- clone | Clone | A family of cells descended from a single ancestor cell. In immunology, all the lymphocytes that share the same receptor (B-cell clones later diversify slightly by mutation).
- cold-tumor | Cold tumor | Informal term for a tumor with few or no T cells inside. Strictly a "desert", though often also used for "excluded" tumors whose T cells are stuck at the edges.
- complement | Complement | A set of dozens of blood proteins that, once triggered on a target's surface (by a microbe, or by antibodies clustered on a cell), set off a cascade of reactions that opsonizes the target for phagocytes, attracts immune cells and can form pores in its membrane.
- costimulation | Costimulation (signal 2) | The confirming second signal a T cell needs, in addition to recognizing its target (signal 1), to become fully activated: the second factor in two-factor authentication. It is classically delivered when CD28 on the T cell binds B7 on a mature dendritic cell; recognition without it can leave the T cell anergic (lastingly unresponsive).
- cross-presentation | Cross-presentation | The ability of certain dendritic cells to present peptides from material they have taken up from outside, for example from a dead tumor cell, on their own MHC class I molecules, so that killer T cells can be primed against it.
- crs | Cytokine release syndrome (CRS) | A body-wide inflammatory reaction, with fever, low blood pressure and sometimes low oxygen or organ damage, set off when a therapy such as CAR-T cells or a T-cell engager activates large numbers of immune cells at once. Much of the main cytokine involved, IL-6, is secreted by the patient's own macrophages and related cells rather than by the T cells. Popularly called a "cytokine storm".
- ctla-4 | CTLA-4 | An inhibitory receptor (a "brake") that T cells express after activation and regulatory T cells express constantly. A close relative of CD28, it binds the same B7 molecules with higher affinity, outcompetes CD28 for them and can remove them from antigen-presenting cells, so it acts mainly while T cells are being primed in lymph nodes; ipilimumab blocks it.
- cytokine | Cytokine | A small signaling protein that cells, especially immune cells, secrete to change the behavior of other cells, for example to make them divide, migrate, attack or suppress a response. Interferons and interleukins are examples.
- dendritic-cell | Dendritic cell | A star-shaped immune cell that takes up antigens in tissues and carries them to a lymph node, where it presents peptides from them on MHC molecules to T cells. Once it has sensed danger, it also expresses the costimulatory molecules needed to activate naive T cells.
- dna | DNA | The long, double-stranded molecule that stores genetic information as a sequence of four bases, written A, C, G and T. Each strand is a chain of nucleotides, and the bases of the two strands pair up, A with T and C with G.
- endoplasmic-reticulum | Endoplasmic reticulum (ER) | A folded internal membrane compartment where a cell assembles proteins destined for its surface. MHC class I molecules are loaded with their peptides there.
- epithelium | Epithelium | A sheet of tightly joined cells that covers the skin and lines the surfaces of organs and body cavities. Carcinomas, the most common cancers, arise from epithelial cells.
- epitope | Epitope | The exact small patch on an antigen that a particular antibody or receptor binds. For a T cell, it is the peptide held in an MHC molecule.
- exhaustion | T-cell exhaustion | A stable state of reduced function that T cells enter under long, unrelenting stimulation, as in chronic infections and tumors. Exhausted cells express many inhibitory receptors such as PD-1, divide little and kill poorly.
- extravasation | Extravasation | The process by which a white blood cell leaves the bloodstream: it rolls along the vessel wall as selectins engage their ligands, arrests when its integrins bind adhesion molecules on the vessel lining, and then migrates out between the lining cells.
- fas | Fas and Fas ligand | A "death receptor" (Fas) found on many cells, and its ligand (Fas ligand), which killer T cells and some tumor blood vessels express; their binding triggers apoptosis in the Fas-bearing cell.
- fc-receptor | Fc receptor | A receptor on immune cells such as macrophages, neutrophils and NK cells that binds the Fc region of antibodies bound to a target, so those cells can attack antibody-coated targets.
- fc-region | Fc region | The stem of an antibody's Y, formed by the lower parts of its two heavy chains' constant regions (Fc stands for "fragment, crystallizable"). It does not bind the target; Fc receptors on immune cells and complement proteins bind it, and its binding to the recycling receptor FcRn determines how long the antibody lasts in the blood.
- gene | Gene | A stretch of DNA whose sequence encodes a protein (or, for some genes, a functional RNA molecule).
- granzyme | Granzyme | A protease (an enzyme that cleaves proteins), released by killer T cells and NK cells, that enters a target cell through perforin pores and triggers apoptosis.
- hazard-ratio | Hazard ratio | A comparison of the rate at which events such as relapse or death happen in two groups at any given moment. 1.0 means no difference; 0.5 means the event happens at half the rate in the first group.
- high-endothelial-venule | High endothelial venule | A specialized small blood vessel in lymph nodes (and some other lymphoid tissue), named for its unusually tall lining cells, where naive lymphocytes leave the blood. Its wall carries the ligands for L-selectin and the chemokines that bind CCR7 on naive T cells.
- hla | HLA (human leukocyte antigen) | The human name for MHC molecules and the genes that encode them. HLA genes are the most variable in the human genome, and each person inherits one set from each parent, so people differ in which peptides their cells can present; this is why transplants must be matched.
- hydrophobic | Hydrophobic and hydrophilic | Hydrophobic (water-repelling) molecules, or parts of molecules, mix poorly with water and cluster together in it. Hydrophilic (water-attracting) ones are charged or polar (carrying slight positive and negative charges) and mix readily with water. The difference shapes cell membranes and drives protein folding.
- ido | IDO (indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase) | An enzyme that converts the amino acid tryptophan into kynurenine; both the tryptophan shortage and the kynurenine suppress T cells.
- ifn-gamma | Interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) | A cytokine secreted mainly by activated T cells and natural killer cells. It makes nearby cells express more MHC molecules, activates macrophages, can halt the growth of some tumor cells and induces PD-L1, the ligand of the inhibitory receptor PD-1.
- il-2 | IL-2 (interleukin-2) | A cytokine that drives T cells to divide; it is used to expand T cells in the laboratory and, at very high doses, can shrink some melanomas and kidney cancers, with severe side effects. Regulatory T cells bind and consume it, which restrains other T cells.
- immune-system | Immune system | The body's network of cells, molecules, tissues and organs that detects and responds to infection, damage and abnormal cells while sparing healthy tissue. It comprises about 1.8 trillion cells in an adult man.
- immunosurveillance | Immunosurveillance | The idea that the immune system continually detects and destroys many newly arising cancer cells before they form tumors. It is well supported in mice, and indirectly in people.
- immunotherapy | Immunotherapy | Treatment that works through the immune system, by strengthening it, releasing its brakes or redirecting it against a disease such as cancer, rather than attacking the disease directly.
- innate-immunity | Innate immunity | The fast, built-in branch of the immune system: barriers, sentinel cells and blood proteins that respond within minutes to hours to broad signs of infection or damage, using receptors inherited in the genome. It does not adapt to a specific invader or form specific memory.
- interferon | Interferon | A family of cytokines named for their ability to interfere with viruses. Type I interferons, secreted by infected cells and some sentinel cells, put neighboring cells into an antiviral state and activate immune cells; interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), made by T cells and NK cells, is a different member.
- jak | JAK1 and JAK2 | Enzymes attached to the inner side of interferon receptors that relay the interferon signal into the cell. Cancer cells that lose them stop responding to interferon-gamma.
- lymph-node | Lymph node | A bean-sized organ, one of hundreds along the lymphatic vessels, to which lymph and dendritic cells carry antigens from nearby tissues, so that the rare T and B cells that recognize them can find them. Most adaptive immune responses begin in lymph nodes.
- lymphocyte | Lymphocyte | A small, round white blood cell. T cells and B cells are the lymphocytes of adaptive immunity; natural killer (NK) cells are innate lymphocytes.
- lymphoma | Lymphoma | A cancer of lymphocytes (most often B cells, sometimes T cells) that usually grows in lymph nodes and other lymphoid tissue. Hodgkin lymphoma is one type.
- lysosome | Lysosome | A membrane-enclosed compartment inside a cell, filled with acid and digestive enzymes, where engulfed or internalized material and worn-out cell components are degraded.
- macrophage | Macrophage | A large immune cell ("big eater") found in nearly every tissue. It engulfs (phagocytoses) microbes, debris, dying cells and antibody-coated cells and secretes cytokines that recruit other immune cells; it can promote or suppress inflammation, and in tumors many macrophages are reprogrammed to suppress immunity.
- melanoma | Melanoma | A cancer of melanocytes, the pigment-producing cells of the skin (and, more rarely, of the eye and mucous membranes). Because sunlight damages DNA, skin melanomas often carry very many mutations.
- memory-cell | Memory cell | A long-lived B or T cell left behind after an immune response, ready to respond faster and more strongly if the same target returns.
- metastasis | Metastasis | The spread of cancer cells from where the tumor began to distant organs, where they form new tumors (metastases). A "metastatic" cancer is one that has spread.
- mhc | MHC (major histocompatibility complex) molecule | A cell-surface molecule that binds protein fragments (peptides) and presents them to T cells. Class I, on nearly every cell with a nucleus, presents peptides from proteins made inside the cell; class II, on antigen-presenting cells, presents peptides from material taken up from outside. In humans, MHC molecules are called HLA.
- mhc-class-i | MHC class I | The MHC molecules on nearly every cell with a nucleus, which present short peptides (usually 8–10 amino acids) from the proteins the cell is making inside, including any viral or mutated ones. They are recognized by killer (CD8) T cells, and their display is nicknamed the cell's "shop window".
- mhc-class-ii | MHC class II | The MHC molecules of professional antigen-presenting cells such as dendritic cells, macrophages and B cells. They present longer peptides (roughly 13–25 amino acids) from whatever reaches the cell's endosomes and lysosomes: material taken up from outside, alongside much of the cell's own protein. They are recognized by helper (CD4) T cells.
- monoclonal-antibody | Monoclonal antibody | Many identical copies of a single antibody, made in the laboratory by a population of cells descended from one cell, so every molecule binds the same target. Antibody drug names have traditionally ended in -mab.
- mrna | Messenger RNA (mRNA) | A short-lived RNA copy of a single gene, which travels from the nucleus to the ribosomes and is translated there into a protein. mRNA vaccines deliver such copies so that the body's own cells make the target protein.
- msi-high | Mismatch-repair deficient (MSI-high) | The state of a tumor whose system for correcting DNA copying errors (mismatch repair) is broken, so uncorrected slips pile up in short repeated stretches of DNA (microsatellites) and throughout its genome. Such tumors carry many mutations, including many frameshifts. Also abbreviated dMMR.
- mutation | Mutation | A change in the DNA sequence, from a single changed base to large rearrangements. Most have no effect, but some alter a protein, and an accumulation of certain mutations can turn a normal cell cancerous.
- naive-t-cell | Naive T cell | A mature T cell that has not yet met its target. Naive T cells circulate between blood and lymph nodes, sampling dendritic cells as they go.
- negative-selection | Negative selection | The test in the thymus that removes developing T cells (thymocytes) whose receptors bind self-peptide–MHC complexes with high affinity: the strong signal triggers their apoptosis. Also called clonal deletion.
- neoantigen | Neoantigen | A peptide produced by a mutated protein in a cancer cell, to which the immune system has never become tolerant. Presented on MHC, it can mark the cell as abnormal to T cells.
- neutrophil | Neutrophil | The most abundant type of immune cell: short-lived, fast-moving and the first to leave the blood for infected tissue, where it engulfs bacteria.
- nk-cell | Natural killer (NK) cell | An innate lymphocyte that kills stressed, infected or cancerous cells without earlier exposure to them, especially cells that have lost MHC class I ("missing self"). It can also kill antibody-coated cells by binding the antibodies' Fc regions with its Fc receptors (ADCC).
- on-target-off-tumor | On-target, off-tumor toxicity | Damage caused by a therapy working exactly as designed, on healthy cells that carry the same target as the tumor. The loss of normal B cells after CD19 CAR-T is the classic example.
- oncogene | Oncogene | A gene whose mutated or overactive form drives cancer by keeping the cell's growth signaling permanently active. Examples include KRAS and HER2.
- oncolytic-virus | Oncolytic virus | A virus, often genetically engineered, that preferentially infects and lyses (bursts) cancer cells, releasing tumor antigens and danger signals that can stimulate an immune response against the tumor.
- opsonization | Opsonization | Coating a microbe or cell with proteins such as antibodies or complement fragments, which receptors on phagocytes bind, so that phagocytes engulf it more readily.
- pd-1 | PD-1 | An inhibitory receptor (a "brake") on activated T cells that stays at high levels when stimulation is prolonged; when it binds its ligands, PD-L1 or PD-L2, on another cell, it inhibits the T cell's activity. It acts mainly in tissues such as tumors, and also in the lymph nodes that drain them; pembrolizumab and nivolumab block it.
- pd-l1 | PD-L1 | The main ligand of PD-1. Many cells express it, especially in response to inflammation and interferon-gamma, and many tumors use it to inhibit the T cells attacking them.
- peptide | Peptide | A short chain of amino acids, such as a fragment cleaved from a larger protein. T cells recognize peptides presented on MHC molecules, not whole proteins.
- peptide-binding-groove | Peptide-binding groove | The cleft along the outer face of an MHC molecule in which a peptide is held for T cells to inspect; pockets in its floor accept the peptide's anchor residues. The class I groove is closed at both ends and holds peptides of 8–10 amino acids, while the class II groove is open at both ends and holds longer ones. Most of the variation between HLA types lies in and around the groove.
- perforin | Perforin | A protein released by killer T cells and natural killer cells that assembles into pores in the target cell's membrane, letting granzymes in.
- plasma-cell | Plasma cell | A fully differentiated B cell that secretes large amounts of a single antibody; some live for decades in the bone marrow.
- proteasome | Proteasome | A barrel-shaped enzyme complex inside every cell that degrades unwanted or faulty proteins by cleaving them into short peptides. Most of these are broken down further to amino acids and recycled; a few are carried into the endoplasmic reticulum by TAP and presented on MHC class I.
- protein | Protein | A chain of amino acids folded into a specific three-dimensional shape. Proteins do most of the work in cells, and their shape determines what they do and what they bind.
- receptor | Receptor | A protein, often spanning the cell membrane, that binds a specific molecule (its ligand) and relays a signal into the cell.
- regulatory-t-cell | Regulatory T cell (Treg) | A type of CD4 T cell, defined by the protein FOXP3, that suppresses other immune cells to prevent autoimmunity. Tumors often recruit them.
- selectin | Selectin | An adhesion molecule whose bonds with its ligands form and break quickly, so that a white blood cell held by selectins rolls along a vessel wall instead of stopping. E- and P-selectin appear on the lining of inflamed vessels and slow passing white blood cells; L-selectin sits on white blood cells, notably naive lymphocytes, which use it to enter lymph nodes through high endothelial venules.
- spleen | Spleen | An organ in the upper left abdomen that filters the blood and hosts large numbers of lymphocytes; it does for the blood what lymph nodes do for the tissues.
- stem-like-t-cell | Stem-like T cell (progenitor exhausted T cell) | A T cell found in chronic infections and tumors, marked by the transcription factor TCF1, that renews itself while giving rise to descendants that kill but become exhausted. It is the main source of new T cells after the PD-1 brake is released.
- t-cell | T cell | A lymphocyte that matures in the thymus (hence "T") and uses its T-cell receptor to recognize peptides presented on MHC molecules. Killer (CD8) T cells destroy infected or cancerous cells; helper (CD4) T cells coordinate the response.
- t-cell-engager | T-cell engager | A two-armed molecule, usually a bispecific antibody, that binds CD3 on a T cell with one arm and a target on a cancer cell with the other, physically bridging the two cells so that the T cell kills the cancer cell.
- tcr | T-cell receptor (TCR) | The recognition molecule on a T cell, assembled by the same gene rearrangement as antibodies (V(D)J recombination) and unique to each T cell and its clone. It recognizes a specific peptide held in a specific MHC (HLA) molecule and, unlike an antibody, is never secreted.
- tcr-t | TCR-T cell | A T cell engineered to express a T-cell receptor cloned from a person whose immune system recognized a particular tumor peptide, often with its affinity increased in the laboratory. Unlike a CAR, it can recognize peptides from proteins made inside the cell, but only in patients with a matching HLA type.
- tgf-beta | TGF-β (transforming growth factor beta) | A cytokine that promotes scarring (fibrosis) and tissue repair and suppresses immune responses. Tumors use it to build fibrous barriers that keep T cells out, and to suppress T cells directly.
- thymus | Thymus | A small organ behind the breastbone, in front of the heart, where T cells develop and are tested so that most self-reactive ones are removed. It is most active early in life and gradually shrinks with age.
- til | Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TIL) | T cells harvested from inside a patient's own tumor, grown to billions in the laboratory and infused back as a treatment. Their receptors are the patient's own and are not engineered.
- tissue-agnostic | Tissue-agnostic approval | Approval of a drug for tumors with a particular molecular feature, such as MSI-high status, regardless of the organ where the cancer began.
- tolerance | Tolerance | The immune system's learned restraint toward the body's own molecules (and some harmless foreign ones, such as food). It is maintained by deleting or silencing self-reactive lymphocytes, both where they develop (central tolerance) and throughout the body (peripheral tolerance).
- tumor | Tumor | An abnormal mass of cells growing beyond the body's normal controls. Benign tumors stay put; malignant ones (cancers) can invade surrounding tissue and spread.
- tumor-associated-macrophage | Tumor-associated macrophage (TAM) | A macrophage living in a tumor, often pushed into a wound-healing ("M2-like") state in which it helps the tumor build blood vessels and suppresses T cells.
- tumor-microenvironment | Tumor microenvironment | Everything in and around a tumor that is not a cancer cell: blood vessels, fibroblasts, immune cells, signaling molecules and the fibers between cells, often arranged in ways that suppress immunity.
- tumor-mutational-burden | Tumor mutational burden (TMB) | The number of mutations in a tumor's DNA, usually counted per million DNA bases (a megabase) sequenced. A high count often, but not always, means more neoantigens, and numbers from different tests are not directly comparable.
- tumor-suppressor | Tumor suppressor | A gene whose normal job is to restrain cell division, repair DNA or trigger apoptosis: a growth brake inside the cell, such as TP53 or RB1. Not to be confused with immune checkpoints, which are brakes on T cells.
- vaccine | Vaccine | A preparation that exposes the immune system to a target in advance, such as a harmless version or piece of a pathogen, or the mRNA to make one, so that the B and T cells that recognize it expand and leave memory cells behind. Cancer vaccines can prevent virus-caused cancers or try to treat existing tumors.
- vegf | VEGF (vascular endothelial growth factor) | A signaling protein that makes blood vessels sprout new branches. Tumors overproduce it to build their blood supply, creating leaky, disorganized vessels that also hinder immune cells.
